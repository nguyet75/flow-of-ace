# Flow State Theory in Sport: Definition, Dimensions, Antecedents, Measurement and Performance

Scope note: these notes cover the core flow literature for designing features in a volleyball pre-match app ("Flow of Ace"). The two books, Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (1990) and Jackson & Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow in Sports* (1999), are copyrighted and were NOT read in full. They are described here from catalog and publisher records and from the peer-reviewed papers and reviews that summarize them. Most findings come from search-engine abstracts and snippets. The session's network blocked full-text fetches from PubMed/PMC, Frontiers, core.ac.uk, Leeds Beckett eprints, the University of Wollongong and Southern Cross University repositories, and Human Kinetics. Figures should be checked against the originals before they appear in a user-facing product. Evidence labels: **[Strong]**, **[Mixed]** and **[Popular/weak]**.

## 1. Definition and the nine dimensions: preconditions vs. characteristics

### Takeaway
Flow is a state of total absorption in a task that is intrinsically rewarding. The classic model has nine dimensions. Three are usually treated as preconditions you can design for: challenge–skill balance, clear goals and unambiguous feedback. The other six describe the experience itself. The split is widely used, but it was never formally tested and scholars still debate it. **[Strong that the nine dimensions are the standard framework; Mixed on the precondition/characteristic split.]**

### Cited Findings
- *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* was first published by Harper & Row, New York, in 1990 (303 pp., ISBN 9780060162535). Harper Perennial later reissued it, including a Modern Classics edition. Csikszentmihalyi first described the concept in *Beyond Boredom and Anxiety* (1975). — [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/title/20392741); [MIT Press Bookstore listing](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780061339202); [World Database of Happiness record](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/flow-the-psychology-of-optimal-experience-441)
- Descriptions of the book present flow as a state of enjoyment, concentration and deep involvement. They say the book argues flow can be cultivated rather than left to chance, mainly by choosing tasks matched to one's ability. — [World Database of Happiness record](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/flow-the-psychology-of-optimal-experience-441); [UChicago Magazine](https://mag.uchicago.edu/education-social-service/whats-game)
- The nine dimensions are:
  1. challenge–skill balance
  2. merging of action and awareness
  3. clear goals
  4. unambiguous feedback
  5. concentration on the task at hand
  6. sense of control
  7. loss of self-consciousness
  8. transformation of time
  9. autotelic experience (intrinsically rewarding)

  — [PMC review listing Csikszentmihalyi 1975/1990](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5973526); [Wikipedia: Flow (psychology)](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_state)
- *Flow in Sports* (Jackson & Csikszentmihalyi, 1999; Human Kinetics) applies the same nine features to athletes. Later reviews cite it as one of the main books behind sport flow research. — [Human Kinetics product page](https://us.humankinetics.com/products/flow-in-sports) (could not be fetched); [Piggott/Swann review of scientific progress in flow](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/4768/1/AReviewofScientificProgressinFlowAM-PIGGOTT.pdf)
- In the sport flow–performance meta-analysis, Harris et al. say the nine dimensions fall into two groups: three proximal conditions (preconditions) for the experience and six that describe the experience itself. — [Harris et al. 2021, via core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/519709932.pdf); [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_the_relationship_between_flow_states_and_performance/29784977)
- Csikszentmihalyi (2014) appears to separate:
  - **Conditions:** clear goals, skill–challenge balance, immediate feedback.
  - **Characteristics:** concentration, merging of action and awareness, loss of reflective self-consciousness, control, time distortion.

  A review notes this split "was never tested in a theoretical model." Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi (2002) and Landhäußer & Keller (2012) also sorted the dimensions into preconditions and components. — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9022035); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7033418)
- One review calls challenge–skill balance "arguably the most important prerequisite" for entering flow. — [Norsworthy et al. 2017, GJSEPER](https://www2.worc.ac.uk/gjseper/documents/Flow_states_and_performance_in_elite_athletes_6_16-28.pdf)
- Critiques:
  - Waterman examined each of the nine original elements to decide whether it is a necessary condition or a subjective feature of flow.
  - A 2020 review argues the state should be clearly separated from the conditions said to produce it.
  - A 2024 metatheory paper says flow research has reached a "crisis point" over the limits of current theoretical explanations.

  — [Flow Theory Re-Envisioned (OUP)](https://academic.oup.com/book/61511); [PMC7033418](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7033418); [Why isn't flow flowing? (SAGE, 2024)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09593543241237492)
- Sources disagree on how many dimensions there are. Some summaries of Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi list six components, while the 1990, 1996 and 1999 works list nine. — [Wikipedia: Flow (psychology)](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_state)

### Inferences
- For the app, the three preconditions are what a pre-match flow can act on:
  - **Clear goals:** process goals for the match or set.
  - **Feedback:** explicit cues for what "good" feels like on each touch.
  - **Challenge–skill balance:** reframing the match as challenging but within reach, and building confidence.
- The six experience dimensions (time distortion, loss of self-consciousness and so on) are better used as items in a post-match self-report than as targets you tell players to aim for. Telling someone to "lose self-consciousness" is likely to backfire.

### Gaps
- I could not check the exact wording in the books or the Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi (2002) chapter, so the assignment of each dimension to condition vs. characteristic is based on secondary sources.

## 2. Jackson's interviews with elite athletes: what facilitates, prevents and disrupts flow, and what athletes see as controllable

### Takeaway
In Jackson's qualitative studies of elite athletes, the main facilitators were:
- physical and mental preparation
- confidence and a positive mental attitude
- appropriate focus
- optimal motivation and arousal
- a performance that feels good and is going well
- for partner and team athletes, unity with partners

The main preventers and disruptors were:
- physical problems or mistakes
- loss of focus
- negative attitude
- a non-supportive environment, such as no audience response

Athletes saw most facilitators and preventers as controllable, but saw disruptors as largely uncontrollable. **[Strong for consistency across qualitative studies; these are interviews, not causal tests.]**

### Cited Findings
- **Jackson (1992)**, *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* 4(2):161–180. She interviewed former US National Champion figure skaters (champions 1985–1990).
  - The abstract says there were 16 skaters, but a later review cites 18. This conflict is unresolved.
  - Factors seen as most important for flow: positive mental attitude, positive pre-competitive and competitive affect, appropriate focus, physical readiness, and unity with partner (pairs and dance).
  - Factors seen as preventing or disrupting flow: physical problems or mistakes, inability to maintain focus, negative mental attitude, and lack of audience response.

  — [T&F reference page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/10413209208406459); [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/c7c2c253768d237d8552063d8ca5735a)
- A systematic review notes that 81% of Jackson's figure-skater sample said flow happened only on rare occasions. — [Norsworthy et al. 2017](https://www2.worc.ac.uk/gjseper/documents/Flow_states_and_performance_in_elite_athletes_6_16-28.pdf)
- **Jackson (1995)**, "Factors influencing the occurrence of flow state in elite athletes," *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* 7(2). Sources give the pages as either 135–163 or 138–166.
  - She interviewed 28 elite athletes from 7 sports.
  - 361 raw themes were grouped into 10 dimensions.
  - The most prominent factors were preparation (physical and mental), confidence, focus, how the performance felt and progressed, and optimal motivation and arousal.

  — [T&F abstract page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413209508406962); [Scilit record](https://www.scilit.net/publications/7a3af1d927d1751096ea06ef6a9f6b86)
- Controllability in Jackson (1995):
  - Most athletes saw flow as controllable.
  - "A large percentage of the factors seen to facilitate or prevent flow were perceived as controllable."
  - Factors that disrupted flow were "largely seen as uncontrollable."

  — [T&F abstract page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413209508406962)
- Jackson's own popular write-up for the Australian Psychological Society (InPsych, 2012) summarizes this work for practitioners. — [APS InPsych, Jackson 2012](https://psychology.org.au/publications/inpsych/2012/december/jackson)
- Later sport flow research uses the same three-way split into factors that facilitate, prevent and disrupt flow. — [Swann et al. "Exploring the interactions underlying flow states" (accepted version)](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/586/2/Exploring%20the%20interactions%20underlying%20flow%20states_ACCEPTED%20VERSION.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Pre-match features should target the controllable facilitators:**
  - a physical and mental preparation checklist and routine
  - confidence building (recalling past successes, highlights)
  - attentional focus cues
  - getting arousal to an optimal level (calming breathing or energizing music, chosen by how the player feels)
  - team or partner unity, which is relevant to volleyball (for example, a short team or pair ritual)
- **Disruptors** (mistakes, refereeing, crowd) are mostly uncontrollable. The app is better used to rehearse a quick reset routine after an error than to promise players they can avoid disruptions.

### Gaps
- I could not see the exact percentages per controllable factor in Jackson (1995). The full text was not reachable.

## 3. Swann et al. (2012) systematic review, flow vs. clutch states, and whether flow can be deliberately triggered

### Takeaway
The 2012 review of 17 studies found:
- some flow dimensions are reported more consistently than others
- the same factors (focus, preparation, motivation, arousal, thoughts and emotions) keep appearing as triggers and blockers
- athletes see flow as controllable "to some extent"

From 2016 onward, Swann's group showed that excellent performance comes from two different states:
- **flow** ("letting it happen"), which tends to arise with open, exploratory goals
- **clutch** ("making it happen"), a deliberate, effortful, high-awareness state that arises with fixed, high-stakes goals

Interventions can raise some flow dimensions. However, a 2023 review found no conclusive evidence that any intervention actually induces flow. **[Strong that flow cannot currently be reliably triggered on demand; Mixed on interventions raising the likelihood.]**

### Cited Findings
- **Swann, Keegan, Piggott & Crust (2012).** "A systematic review of the experience, occurrence, and controllability of flow states in elite sport." *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 13(6):807–819, doi:10.1016/j.psychsport.2012.05.006.
  - It reviewed 17 empirical studies published from 1992 to 2011, and the findings were mainly qualitative.
  - Some dimensions were experienced more consistently than others.
  - Key factors were consistently reported to induce or inhibit flow.
  - Flow was seen as "controllable to some extent rather than purely coincidental."
  - Physiology appears relevant, so flow may be a psychophysiological state.

  — [Leeds Beckett eprint record](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/273); [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/sspapers/2417)
- A later paper by the same group lists focus, preparation, motivation, arousal, and thoughts and emotions as factors that facilitate, prevent or disrupt flow across sports. — [Swann et al., Exploring the interactions underlying flow states](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/586/2/Exploring%20the%20interactions%20underlying%20flow%20states_ACCEPTED%20VERSION.pdf)
- A 2017 review attributes to the 2012 review a figure that 72% of elite athletes saw getting into flow as within their control. **This could not be checked against the 2012 full text.** — [Norsworthy et al. 2017](https://www2.worc.ac.uk/gjseper/documents/Flow_states_and_performance_in_elite_athletes_6_16-28.pdf)
- **Swann, Keegan, Crust & Piggott (2016).** *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 23:101–113.
  - Professional golfers interviewed after excellent performances described two states:
    - "letting it happen," which matched flow
    - "making it happen," which was "more effortful and intense, involved a heightened awareness of the situation, and therefore differed to flow"

  — [Leeds Beckett accepted manuscript](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/2596/2/Psychological%20states%20underlying%20excellent%20performance%20in%20professional%20golfers.pdf); [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Psychological_states_underlying_excellent_performance_in_professional_golfers_Letting_it_happen_vs_making_it_happen_/27738651)
- **Swann, Crust, Jackman, Vella, Allen & Keegan (2017).** "Psychological states underlying excellent performance in sport: Toward an integrated model of flow and clutch states." *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* 29(4):375–401.
  - It studied 26 athletes across team, net/wall, endurance and other sports, from world-class to recreational level.
  - They were interviewed about 4 days after an excellent performance.
  - Flow and clutch were distinct states, "each of which occurred through separate contexts and processes," and athletes moved between the two states during a performance.

  — [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/sspapers/2917); [T&F](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/permissions/10.1080/10413200.2016.1272650?scroll=top)
- **Clutch study (Journal of Sports Sciences, 2017).** It examined 16 athletes and found 12 characteristics of clutch states, including heightened and deliberate concentration, intense effort and heightened awareness. These set clutch apart from flow. — [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/sspapers/2911)
- **Swann, Crust & Vella (2017),** *Current Opinion in Psychology.* It argues that 'open' goals (exploratory, "see how well I can do") go with flow, while 'fixed' goals (specific outcomes that must be achieved) go with clutch. — [UOW record "New directions… flow and clutch states"](https://ro.uow.edu.au/sspapers/3024)
- **Jackman et al. (2021),** recreational distance running. Flow was helped by intrinsic, experiential motives and non-specific goals. — [Ulster accepted version](https://pure.ulster.ac.uk/files/100069092/Jackman_et_al._2021_Integrated_model_and_self_regulation.pdf)
- The group also developed and gave preliminary validation to a Flow-Clutch Scale. — [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/test2021/3446)
- **Norsworthy, Gorczynski & Jackson (2017),** systematic review of flow training in elite athletes.
  - The evidence base was 6 pre-experimental single-subject studies and 1 experimental study (2005–2014).
  - Interventions were hypnosis, imagery, music, and client-centred pre-performance routines.
  - All reported positive effects on flow and performance, but the study designs were weak.

  — [GJSEPER PDF](https://www2.worc.ac.uk/gjseper/documents/Flow_states_and_performance_in_elite_athletes_6_16-28.pdf); [Portsmouth record](https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/publications/a-systematic-review-of-flow-training-on-flow-states-and-performan/)
- **2023 systematic review of flow interventions in sport and exercise** (*International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, Southern Cross University-affiliated authors). Its key statements:
  - Some interventions increased certain flow dimensions.
  - "No studies to date have reported conclusive evidence that flow was induced through an intervention."
  - Interventions so far have "largely been unsuccessful at producing flow experiences."

  — [SCU research portal record](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-systematic-review-of-flow-interventions/991012942199702368)
- **Garzón Mosquera & Araya Vargas (2020),** meta-analysis of imagery and hypnosis interventions (17 studies).
  - Effect size on flow: 0.782. Effect size on performance: 0.339.
  - The authors concluded imagery is the most effective technique.
  - This is a smaller, less rigorous review.

  — [Revista Pensar en Movimiento](https://revistas.ucr.ac.cr/index.php/rpem/article/view/11114); [SciELO PDF](https://www.scielo.sa.cr/pdf/pem/v18n1/1659-4436-pem-18-1-00031.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Product framing:** say "raise the odds of flow," not "trigger flow." The evidence supports making flow more likely (preparation, confidence, focus, arousal regulation, imagery, music, routines). It does not support switching flow on reliably.
- **Volleyball relevance of flow vs. clutch:**
  - Volleyball has many high-pressure fixed-goal moments: serving at 23–24, set point. Those moments call for clutch, a deliberate, effortful focus.
  - Open, exploratory phases favour flow.
  - The app could teach both, for example "play freely" cues for open phases and a "make it happen" routine for pressure points.
- **Goal-setting design:** offer open, exploratory goals before a match (for example, "see how many aggressive serves I can land") as well as fixed outcome goals. This follows the open-goal → flow finding.

### Gaps
- I could not check the full 2023 review: number of studies, author list and the specific interventions.
- I found no RCT showing that a pre-match app increases flow.

## 4. Measuring flow (FSS-2, DFS-2, short scales) and an option for in-app self-tracking

### Takeaway
The standard sport measures are:
- the 36-item **Flow State Scale-2** (FSS-2; state flow during a specific event)
- the 36-item **Dispositional Flow Scale-2** (DFS-2; how often a person typically experiences flow)
- their **9-item short forms** (one item per dimension)

All were validated by Jackson, Martin & Eklund (2008), and translations have been validated in several languages. The 9-item Short FSS-2 is the best evidence-based choice for a quick post-match check-in. It is copyrighted and licensed, so check usage rights. **[Strong for psychometric validity; note copyright.]**

### Cited Findings
- **Jackson, Martin & Eklund (2008).** "Long and short measures of flow: the construct validity of the FSS-2, DFS-2, and new brief counterparts." *Journal of Sport & Exercise Psychology* 30(5):561–587.
  - Samples: dispositional, n=652 (long) and n=692 (short); state, n=499 (long) and n=865 (short).
  - The long forms have 36 items. The short forms have 9 items.
  - "With few exceptions," the measures showed acceptable model fit, reliability and distributions, expected associations with related measures, and invariant factor loadings.

  — [PubMed 18971512](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18971512/); [Stirling record](https://stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/641805)
- The FSS-2 revised the original FSS by replacing five items (Jackson & Eklund). — [Brazilian FSS-2 validation including volleyball players](https://lume.ufrgs.br/bitstream/10183/203951/1/001095817.pdf)
- **Translated short-form validations:**
  - **Turkish Short FSS-2 (Çağlar et al., 2020; second study n=423 athletes, confirmatory factor analysis).** Judged valid and reliable for sport. — [Studia Psychologica via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/0a4ee64f7924415281617f6071d8f942)
  - **Brazilian-Portuguese FSS-BR-S (PLoS ONE 2024, n=396, not athletes only).** Good internal consistency. — [PMC10833536](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10833536/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/7b00a95ed4a04b30bbebafed34d5c28e)
  - **Colombian Flow State Short Scale (333 athletes).** Reliability above 0.8. — [Cuadernos de Psicología del Deporte](https://revistas.um.es/cpd/article/download/619791/387291/2684011)
- There is a separate 7-item short flow scale (Sánchez Vara et al., 2023, *Frontiers in Psychology*). It should not be confused with the 9-item Short FSS-2. — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1066494/full)
- **Measurement criticisms:**
  - A review of 42 studies found flow measured in 24 different ways, and argues most of these "miss the mark."
  - A 2024 *Communications Psychology* paper says experimental flow-induction methods have changed little since the concept was introduced.

  — [PMC10360049](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10360049/); [Communications Psychology 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s44271-024-00115-3)

### Inferences
- **App design for self-tracking:**
  - Ask a 9-item post-match check-in, one item per dimension, on a 1–5 scale.
  - Optionally ask a 1–3 item "pulse" right after a match (for example: absorption, sense of control, enjoyment).
  - Over time, compare flow scores with the pre-match routine used and with self-rated performance.
- The official Short FSS-2 items are under copyright, through Mind Garden and the authors. The app needs a licence, or it should use its own item wording that is not presented as the validated scale.
- **Measure flow after the match, not during it.** Asking during play would itself break flow.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the current licensing terms or price for the FSS-2 and DFS-2 short forms.
- I could not get Cronbach's alpha values for the original English short scales.

## 5. Kotler / Flow Research Collective "flow triggers" and the "flow cycle": evidence vs. popular claims

### Takeaway
Kotler's "flow triggers" started as a list of 17 and are now cited as 22. Many repackage the classic preconditions (challenge–skill balance, clear goals, immediate feedback, concentration) and add environmental and social items (risk, novelty, "deep embodiment," group flow). The "flow cycle" (struggle → release → flow → recovery) and the neurochemical explanations come from popular books and a 2022 theoretical review co-authored by Kotler. They have not been tested experimentally as a system. **[Popular claim with weak support; the parts that overlap with Csikszentmihalyi's preconditions do have support.]**

### Cited Findings
- Popular sources say Kotler and colleagues have identified "at least 22" flow triggers.
- An older version had 17 triggers:
  - psychological: 4
  - environmental: 3
  - social: 9
  - creative: 1

  — [Gridfiti summary](https://gridfiti.com/flow-state-triggers/); [Big Think interview with Kotler](https://bigthink.com/videos/understanding-flow-triggers-with-steven-kotler/); [Elitefts "17 triggers"](https://elitefts.com/education/17-triggers-to-induce-flow)
- Kotler's teaching material calls challenge–skill balance the most critical trigger and describes triggers as preconditions that lead to more flow. — [CreativeLive lesson](https://site.creativelive.com/class/get-into-your-creative-flow-steven-kotler/lessons/flow-triggers)
- For group flow, Kotler draws on Keith Sawyer's list (shared goals, close listening, and others). — [Open Culture, 2025](https://www.openculture.com/2025/04/how-to-enter-a-flow-state-on-command.html)
- **Kotler, Mannino, Kelso & Huskey (2022),** "First few seconds for flow: A comprehensive proposal of the neurobiology and neurodynamics of state onset," *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* 143:104956.
  - It is a theoretical proposal involving large-scale brain networks and dopamine, noradrenaline and endocannabinoid systems.
  - It calls triggers pre-conditions "proposed to produce, or at least be correlated with, flow."

  — [stevenkotler.com paper page](https://stevenkotler.com/papers/neuroscience-and-biobehavioral-review-2022)
- **Neuroscience evidence is unsettled.** Some studies support "transient hypofrontality" (reduced prefrontal activity), while others propose increased synchronization. One review calls the evidence "sparse and inconclusive." — [PMC12827708](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827708/); [Communications Psychology 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s44271-024-00115-3)
- I found no primary Flow Research Collective page listing all 22 triggers, and no peer-reviewed test of the "flow cycle." — [search results summarized above; see Gridfiti/Big Think]

### Inferences
- Kotler's vocabulary ("flow triggers," "struggle phase," "recovery") makes good UX copy, provided the app does not claim that it "triggers flow on command" or that neurochemical effects are established. Present it as "evidence-informed habits that raise the odds."
- **Triggers worth using in a volleyball app**, because they overlap with peer-reviewed antecedents:
  - clear goals
  - immediate feedback
  - challenge–skill balance
  - complete concentration (removing distractions, phone on Do Not Disturb)
  - shared goals and close listening between teammates

### Gaps
- The "flow cycle" (struggle–release–flow–recovery) comes from Kotler's popular books (*The Rise of Superman*, *Stealing Fire*, *The Art of Impossible*). I did not find peer-reviewed empirical support for it, and these notes cite no primary source for it.

## 6. Volleyball-specific flow research and team flow

### Takeaway
Volleyball-specific flow research is small, mostly cross-sectional, and often unpublished theses. It suggests flow in volleyball goes with:
- more experience and training
- higher self-confidence and self-efficacy
- lower anxiety
- task orientation
- team cohesion

Team flow research (van den Hout) comes mostly from organizations, with some hockey consulting. **[Mixed/weak for volleyball; Mixed for team flow in sport.]**

### Cited Findings
- **Dispositional flow, 506 athletes (volleyball, athletics, tennis), Turkish DFS-2.** Volleyball players with more years in the sport and more weekly training reported higher dispositional flow. — [search summary of CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/322551259.pdf)
- **Thai professional league, 16 female players.** Flow had a weak negative relationship with state anxiety. Flow, self-confidence and anxiety were related to performance outcomes in competition. — [JSST (Thai journal)](https://he01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JSST/article/download/192079/156599/772225)
- **Greek thesis, 190 volleyball athletes (90 men, 100 women; national B and A2 divisions).** FSS-2 given right after competition. Anxiety, self-confidence and social cohesion predicted flow and performance. — [University of Athens thesis](https://pergamos.lib.uoa.gr/uoa/dl/frontend/en/browse/2875841)
- **Brazilian male volleyball players.** Flow related positively to perceived ability, self-efficacy and task orientation. — [Revista da Educação Física/UEM](https://periodicos.uem.br/ojs/index.php/RevEducFis/article/view/17024); [UFJF thesis "Quando o jogo flui"](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsndl,edsndl.IBICT.oai.hermes.cpd.ufjf.br.ufjf.4508)
- **Koehn (2012), confidence and anxiety as predictors of flow in competition** (not volleyball-only). — [Liverpool Hope repository](https://hira.hope.ac.uk/4525/1/Koehn%202012%20Effects%20of%20confidence%20and%20anxiety%20on%20flow%20state%20in%20competition.pdf)
- **Team flow model (van den Hout, Davis & Weggeman 2018, *Journal of Psychology*).**
  - It starts with a collective ambition.
  - Its experiential features are a sense of unity, a sense of joint progress, mutual trust and holistic focus.
  - It names seven prerequisites, including collective ambition and mutual trust.

  — [T&F](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00223980.2018.1449729); [Springer 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s41042-021-00059-7)
- **Team Flow Monitor intervention, 80 teams.** Average team flow scores rose over the intervention, but the setting was mostly organizations. — [HAL, "How to spark team flow over time" (2024)](https://hal.univ-lille.fr/hal-04689446v1)
- Van den Hout's practice includes field hockey teams (Den Bosch, the Dutch women's team, the Spanish national team). — [Boekenbank book sample](https://api.boekenbank.be/sample_content/9789/461/561/9789461561886.pdf)
- **"Shared flow" in collective sport.** One study links shared flow in collective physical activities with subjective well-being. — [EJIHPE](https://dx.doi.org/10.1989/ejihpe.v6i3.176)
- **Jackson (1992):** for pairs and dance skaters, "unity with partner" was a key facilitator. This is relevant to beach volleyball pairs. — [T&F](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/10413209208406459)

### Inferences
- **Team features could include:**
  - a shared team or pair goal set before the match (collective ambition)
  - a short trust and communication ritual
  - a "joint progress" check-in between sets
- Beach pairs map most closely onto Jackson's partner-unity finding.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous (experimental or longitudinal) volleyball flow study from 2018–2026 and no peer-reviewed team flow study in volleyball. Several of the volleyball sources above are theses or small regional journals.

## 7. Is flow associated with better sport performance?

### Takeaway
Yes, moderately. The best meta-analysis (Harris et al., 2021) found a medium positive correlation of about r = .31 between flow and performance across sport and gaming tasks. The direction of causality is unclear: good performance may produce flow as much as flow produces good performance. **[Strong for association; weak for causation.]**

### Cited Findings
- **Harris, Allen, Vine & Wilson (2021),** *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*.
  - It covered 20 articles (22 studies), with a mean study-quality score of 76.5% (SD 9.7).
  - Pooled r = 0.31 (95% CI 0.24–0.38).
  - The authors say current evidence cannot determine the nature of the flow–performance relationship, and that none of the proposed mechanisms is "supported by convincing empirical evidence."

  — [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_the_relationship_between_flow_states_and_performance/29784977); [T&F](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1929402)
- **Garzón Mosquera & Araya Vargas (2020).** The flow–performance correlation was ρ = .312, consistent with Harris et al. — [Revista Pensar en Movimiento](https://revistas.ucr.ac.cr/index.php/rpem/article/view/11114)
- **Swann (2016, golf) and Swann (2017, integrated model).** Excellent performances are also reached through clutch, not only flow, so flow is not the only route to peak performance. — [UOW](https://ro.uow.edu.au/sspapers/2917)
- **Thai volleyball study.** Flow, self-confidence and anxiety were linked to competition performance outcomes, in a small sample of 16. — [JSST](https://he01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JSST/article/download/192079/156599/772225)

### Inferences
- r ≈ .31 means flow accounts for roughly 10% of the variance in performance (r² ≈ .096; my calculation). It matters, but it is not decisive.
- The app should present flow mainly as a desirable, enjoyable optimal experience that tends to go with good play. It should not guarantee better results.
- Tracking flow alongside a performance self-rating in the app could give users personal correlations, with the caveat that the direction is ambiguous.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analytic effect size specific to volleyball or team sports.
- There is a systematic review of flow in elite team sports for 2015–2020, but I could not read it. — [search result: ECU/other repository](https://thescholarship.ecu.edu/bitstreams/10e146a1-3262-4e40-b6ed-e724a59eabb5/download)
- I did not research how eye–hand coordination relates to flow. That is outside this note's scope.
