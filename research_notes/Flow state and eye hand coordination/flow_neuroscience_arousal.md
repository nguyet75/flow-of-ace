Method note (2026-11-23): about 22 searches. Full-text fetches failed because PMC, Frontiers, uni-trier.de and med.stanford.edu were not reachable from this session. **Findings come from abstracts, search-engine summaries of abstracts, and repository records, not from full texts.** Numbers marked "(abstract/summary)" should be checked against the full paper before they go into the app. Evidence grades: **Strong** = several meta-analyses or replications agree; **Mixed** = some support with conflicting or small studies; **Weak/Popular** = hypothesis, single small study, or popular-press claim.

## 1. Transient hypofrontality (Dietrich 2003/2004): claim and test results

### Takeaway
Dietrich proposed that flow requires a temporary drop in prefrontal (explicit, effortful) activity. The direct neuroimaging tests are equivocal. Some studies show a decrease in medial PFC, but others show increases in lateral and inferior frontal regions. **Grade: Weak/Mixed.** The app should not say "your prefrontal cortex switches off in flow."

### Cited Findings
- Dietrich (2004, *Consciousness and Cognition*) proposed that "a necessary prerequisite to the experience of flow is a state of transient hypofrontality" that lets implicit (non-conscious) processing run skilled behaviour — [PubMed 15522630](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15522630/)
- The exercise version of the hypothesis (Dietrich & Audiffren) holds that heavy motor, sensory and autonomic demand produces a concomitant transient decrease of PFC activity — [PubMed 17081621](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17081621/)
- A systematic review and meta-analysis on exercise found PFC-dependent cognition impaired during high-intensity exercise (β = −0.81, 95% CI −1.60 to −0.02, p = .04). This is indirect support and concerns exercise, not flow — [PMC8702964](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8702964)
- Harris, Vine & Wilson (2017, *Progress in Brain Research*, "Neurocognitive mechanisms of the flow state") flag "equivocal neuroimaging findings supporting prominent accounts of hypofrontality." They report that results point instead to a role for higher-order attentional networks, and they note a dissociation between psychophysiological and experiential measures — [PubMed 29031465](https://www.pubmed.gov/29031465/); [Exeter record](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/30805)
- Ulrich et al. found decreased medial PFC (and amygdala) activity during flow, which is partly consistent with the hypothesis, but *increased* left inferior frontal gyrus activity, which is not "global" hypofrontality — [PubMed 23959200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23959200/)
- A 2026 *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience* review describes the field as inconsistent: some studies suggest prefrontal suppression in line with the hypothesis, and others suggest enhanced network synchronization (search summary) — [PMC12827708](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827708/)
- Much "hypofrontality" literature concerns resting hypofrontality in schizophrenia. Task-related hypofrontality has not been consistently replicated there, so that work does not transfer to flow — [Wikipedia: Hypofrontality](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Hypofrontality) (secondary)
- Wolf et al. (2014, table tennis EEG during motor imagery): experts showed stronger 8–10 Hz motor-cortex ERD, and the authors suggest "less activation of the fronto-parietal attention network" may characterise world-class players. Flow was not measured in that abstract. Later papers cite a Wolf et al. 2015 study for reduced verbal-analytic (left temporal) influence in elite players "experiencing flow," but I could not verify the 2015 paper — [Frontiers 10.3389/fnbeh.2014.00370](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2014.00370); [PMC8205149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8205149)

### Inferences
- The defensible version is narrower: during flow, activity in *self-referential/medial* prefrontal regions (DMN-linked) tends to drop, and verbal-analytic control of movement appears reduced in experts. "Prefrontal shutdown" overstates the evidence.
- This fits the choking literature in Section 7: explicit step-by-step monitoring hurts automated skills.

### Gaps
- Dietrich (2003, "Functional neuroanatomy of altered states of consciousness") was not retrieved directly.
- I found no pre-registered test designed specifically to falsify transient hypofrontality in flow.

## 2. fMRI/EEG flow studies and recent reviews

### Takeaway
Flow is associated with reduced activity in medial PFC/DMN and amygdala, with recruitment of attention, executive and reward/striatal regions, and with frontal theta plus moderate alpha in EEG. The 2022 systematic review (Alameda et al.) concludes that the neural correlates of flow **cannot yet be determined**. **Grade: Mixed (small samples, lab tasks such as arithmetic and video games, not sport).**

### Cited Findings
- **Ulrich et al. 2014** (perfusion fMRI, n = 27, adaptive mental arithmetic vs. boredom and overload): flow → relative *increases* in left anterior IFG and left putamen, and relative *decreases* in medial PFC and amygdala. Subjective flow ratings were associated with IFG and amygdala changes, with a trend for MPFC — [PubMed 23959200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23959200/)
- **Ulrich, Keller & Grön 2016** (*SCAN*, BOLD block design) largely replicated those patterns — [SCAN 11(3):496](https://academic.oup.com/scan/article/11/3/496/2375154); [PMC4769635](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4769635)
- **Klasen et al. 2012** (*SCAN*, n = 13 young males, first-person shooter, 12 min): game events coded on five flow factors were linked to somatosensory and motor network activation, which the authors read as evidence for the role of simulation. The study coded *situations likely to produce flow* rather than measuring experienced flow — [PubMed 21596764](https://www.pubmed.gov/21596764/); [SCAN 7(4):485](https://academic.oup.com/scan/article/7/4/485/1736676); [Discover commentary](https://www.discovermagazine.com/mind/how-your-brain-gets-in-the-game)
- A video-game review summarises that a difficulty–skill balance corresponds with activation in attentional (lateral PFC, cerebellum, thalamus, SPL) and reward (caudate, nucleus accumbens, putamen) structures (Klasen 2012; Yoshida 2014) — [PMC7751419](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7751419/)
- **Katahira et al. 2018** (EEG, n = 16, arithmetic boredom/flow/overload): flow showed increased frontal theta *and* moderate frontal/central alpha. Frontal theta was also elevated in overload, so theta alone does not separate flow from strain — [Frontiers 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00300](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00300/full); [PubMed 29593605](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29593605/)
- **Harris, Vine & Wilson 2017**: flow has had "only preliminary investigation" from a neuroscientific perspective, and results point to higher-order attentional networks — [PubMed 29031465](https://www.pubmed.gov/29031465/)
- **van der Linden, Tops & Bakker 2021a** (*Eur J Neurosci*, "Go with the flow"): a network model in which flow involves reduced default mode network activity (low self-referential thinking) — [PubMed 33084102](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33084102/)
- **van der Linden, Tops & Bakker 2021b** (*Frontiers in Psychology*, mini-review): proposes the locus coeruleus–norepinephrine (LC-NE) system as a substrate. Flow relates to arousal in an inverted U, LC-NE theory predicts the highest task engagement at intermediate arousal, and skill–challenge match matters for both. The authors state that the neurocognitive processes of flow "remain largely unclear" — [PMC7983950](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7983950); [Erasmus record](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/the-neuroscience-of-the-flow-state-involvement-of-the-locus-coeru/)
- **Alameda, Sanabria & Ciria 2022** (*Cortex* 154:348–364, systematic review): 25 studies, 471 participants (EEG, fMRI, fNIRS, tDCS), with risk of bias assessed by RoB 2. Studies point to attention, executive and reward systems, with DLPFC, MPFC and IFG prominent, but "the dynamics of these brain regions during flow state are inconsistent." Evidence is sparse and inconclusive, and per one summary "it is not possible to determine the neural correlates … or even the existence of the phenomenon itself" — [UGR repository](https://digibug.ugr.es/handle/10481/78493); [preprint PDF](https://digibug.ugr.es/bitstream/10481/78493/1/Flow_Review%20PRE%20PRINT.pdf); [CIMCYC news](https://cimcyc.ugr.es/en/information/news/brain-flow-systematic-review-neural-basis-flow-stat); [PubMed 35926367](https://www.pubmed.gov/35926367/)
- The 2026 Frontiers review again flags small samples, difficulty inducing flow reliably, and reliance on indirect flow measures (search summary) — [PMC12827708](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827708/)

### Inferences
- The most consistent finding is lower self-referential (MPFC/DMN) activity and lower amygdala (threat) activity, with *active* attentional control. This supports app copy such as "absorbed, less self-talk, less threat," not "brain off."
- Nearly all imaging is in seated lab tasks. Nothing found images flow during an open-skill team sport like volleyball.

### Gaps
- I found no EEG neurofeedback evidence showing that inducing a theta/alpha pattern *causes* flow.
- Exact effect sizes from Ulrich and Katahira were not retrieved.

## 3. Dopamine / norepinephrine / anandamide "neurochemical cocktail" (Kotler)

### Takeaway
The claim that flow releases a cascade of norepinephrine, dopamine, anandamide, serotonin and endorphins is **Popular/Weak**. I found no direct human measurement of anandamide or dopamine *during flow*. A dopaminergic/noradrenergic role is a reasonable theoretical hypothesis that peer-reviewed reviews present as a proposal.

### Cited Findings
- Kotler describes the five-chemical cascade in talks and says "we believe flow evolved from runner's high" — [Big Think video](https://bigthink.com/videos/the-neurochemistry-of-flow-states-with-steven-kotler/)
- His peer-reviewed paper (Kotler, Mannino, Kelso & Huskey 2022, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* 143:104956) is explicitly a "comprehensive *proposal*" of onset neurobiology, drawing on dopaminergic, noradrenergic and endocannabinoid systems and large-scale networks — [author page](https://stevenkotler.com/papers/neuroscience-and-biobehavioral-review-2022); [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/42bW65j4)
- The 2026 Frontiers review lists elucidating neurochemical underpinnings "(e.g., dopamine release)" as a *future direction* (search summary) — [PMC12827708](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827708/)
- The LC-NE hypothesis (van der Linden et al. 2021) is theory-based and draws on LC-NE/arousal literature rather than on direct NE measurement during flow — [PMC7983950](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7983950)
- A non-peer-reviewed critique calls the Flow Genome Project's claims of six-neurotransmitter involvement unsupported (blog) — [Deric Bownds MindBlog 2017](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2017/11/modern-flimflam-men-flow-genome-project.html?m=0)
- Striatal/putamen and reward-region activation in fMRI (Ulrich; Klasen; Yoshida) is *indirect*, consistent with dopaminergic involvement but not a measurement of dopamine — [PubMed 23959200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23959200/); [PMC7751419](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7751419/)

### Inferences
- The app should avoid "flood your brain with dopamine/anandamide" copy. Safer wording: "Researchers think reward and arousal systems are involved, but this hasn't been measured directly."

### Gaps
- I found no PET, microdialysis or blood-endocannabinoid study measuring these chemicals during verified flow in humans.

## 4. Psychophysiology: arousal, HRV, cortisol and flow

### Takeaway
Flow appears to sit at **moderate** sympathetic/HPA arousal (inverted U) rather than at calm or high stress. HRV findings conflict across studies. **Grade: Mixed (small samples).**

### Cited Findings
- **Peifer, Schulz, Schächinger, Baumann & Antoni 2014** (*J Exp Soc Psychol*; n = 22 healthy men; modified Trier Social Stress Test, then a 60-min complex computer task). Abstract-level summary: flow showed an **inverted-U relationship with sympathetic arousal indices and cortisol**, and **parasympathetic HRV indices correlated linearly and positively** with flow. The authors conclude that moderate sympathetic arousal and HPA activation, possibly with co-activation of both autonomic branches, characterise flow — [Leuphana record](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/the-relation-of-flow-experience-and-physiological-arousal-under-s/); [Lübeck record](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/the-relation-of-flow-experience-and-physiological-arousal-under-s/)
  - Discrepancy: a later review table summarises it as inverted U for **LF-HRV** and cortisol, and positive linear for **HF-HRV** — [PMC7751419 table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7751419/table/table-1). Check the original before citing HRV specifics.
- **Tozman et al. 2015** (n = 19, 6-min racing game, ECG), per a review table: negative linear LF-HRV–flow relation in the flow condition, and an inverted U for LF/HF-HRV only in the anxiety (over-challenge) condition. Another review describes a different game, so details are unresolved — [PMC7751419 table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7751419/table/table-1); [PMC5495833](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5495833)
- A study titled "Is There an Optimal Autonomic State for Enhanced Flow and Executive Task Performance?" addresses the same question — [PubMed 31474898](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31474898/) (results not retrieved)
- Rácz et al. 2025 (*Sci Rep*) reportedly replicated a U-shaped HRV characteristic, and a 2026 VR study reported associations of HF-HRV and LF/HF with perceived flow (search summary) — [Rácz et al. 2025 PDF](https://real.mtak.hu/236715/1/Racz_et_al_Sci_Rep_2025.pdf); [PubMed 41947271](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41947271/)
- The LC-NE model also predicts an inverted U between arousal and flow — [PMC7983950](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7983950)

### Inferences
- For the app, the target is *moderate activation*, not maximal relaxation. A "down-regulation" routine that drives an anxious player to very low arousal could overshoot. This supports aiming down-regulation at "steady/ready" rather than "calm/sleepy."
- Higher vagal tone (HF-HRV) may coexist with flow alongside moderate sympathetic activation, which fits slow breathing (raising vagal HRV) for anxious players. This is an inference, not tested in sport.

### Gaps
- No sport-field (in-match) studies of HRV/cortisol and flow were found.
- Effect sizes and curve parameters of the inverted U were not available. Nobody has given a numeric "optimal HRV/HR" target, so the app should not invent one.

## 5. Arousal–performance models: inverted U, IZOF, catastrophe theory; how individual is the optimum?

### Takeaway
A single universal inverted U is too simple. The IZOF meta-analysis supports **individual** optimal zones. Catastrophe models get partial support, mostly for hysteresis: once performance collapses under high cognitive anxiety plus high arousal, small arousal reductions don't restore it. Self-confidence predicts performance better than anxiety does. **Grade: Mixed overall; individual zones moderately supported.**

### Cited Findings
- **IZOF meta-analysis** (Jokela & Hanin 1999, *J Sports Sci* 17:873–887): 19 studies (1978–1997), 146 effect sizes, 6,387 participants. It tested whether athletes inside their personal optimal anxiety zone outperform those outside. A search summary reports in-zone athletes performing about half a SD better; that magnitude is unverified — [JYU record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstreams/306dfd29-45aa-4514-a2ea-5ce9e3993c15/download); [citation listing](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/02640410802027352)
- Ruiz, Raglin & Hanin 2017 (*Int J Sport Exerc Psychol* 15(1):41–63) review the IZOF model 1978–2014 and identify 183 IZOF-based publications — [informahealthcare](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/1612197X.2015.1041545); [BISp record](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201702001346)
- A UCLan paper extends IZOF to team-level "shared zones of optimal functioning," relevant to volleyball — [2019 PDF](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/33462/1/2019_Shared-Zones-of-Optimal-Functioning_clok.pdf)
- **Woodman & Hardy 2003** meta-analysis (k = 48): cognitive anxiety r = −0.10, self-confidence r = 0.24 (confidence effect significantly larger) — [PubMed 12846532](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12846532/)
- **Craft et al. 2003** (*JSEP* 25(1):44–65), CSAI-2 meta-analysis, as summarised by a later review: self-confidence r = 0.25, cognitive anxiety r = 0.01, somatic anxiety r = −0.03. Relationships were stronger in individual than team sports — [metadat dataset](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/metadat/html/dat.craft2003.html); [MDPI review](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/14/8/334)
- **Catastrophe model** (Hardy): under low cognitive anxiety, arousal–performance follows an inverted U, and under high cognitive anxiety, high arousal can produce sudden drops. One review concludes the evidence supports hysteresis and partially supports the interaction prediction. Performance may be maintained at an increased physiological cost, and cognitive anxiety may increase effort — [Bangor record](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/files/20572479/null)
- A Bangor thesis suggests that catastrophe findings may reflect a cognitive anxiety × effort interaction rather than cognitive anxiety × physiological arousal — [Edwards PhD 1998](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/files/42545401/T_EDWARDS_PhD_1998_OCR.pdf)

### Inferences
- The app should treat the optimal zone as **per-player and learnable**, for example by logging pre-match state against self-rated performance and flow over matches, and should not hard-code one target.
- Because confidence predicts performance more reliably than anxiety level does, interpretation ("this is readiness") may matter as much as lowering arousal.
- Hysteresis suggests intervening *before* the player tips over, which favours a pre-match routine and between-rally resets over trying to recover after a collapse.

### Gaps
- I found no volleyball-specific IZOF or catastrophe data.
- The precise IZOF effect size needs verification from the full text.

## 6. Acute effects of slow paced breathing, cyclic sighing, box breathing and music tempo

### Takeaway
Slow breathing reliably raises vagally mediated HRV during and just after practice (**Strong** for physiology). Its effects on anxiety are small to moderate and less robust (**Mixed**). Evidence that one acute session improves *sport performance* is **Weak**. Cyclic sighing beat mindfulness for mood in one RCT, but a replication poster failed on mood (**Mixed/Weak**). Music has small but reliable effects on affect and performance. Fast, loud pre-task music raises arousal and sped reaction time in one tennis study (**Strong** for general effects, **Weak–Mixed** for tempo-specific up-regulation in skill sports).

### Cited Findings
**Slow paced breathing (~6/min)**
- Laborde et al. 2022 (*Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 138:104711) is a systematic review and meta-analysis of 223 studies (172 measuring during breathing, 16 immediately after one session, 49 after multi-session interventions). It reports vmHRV increases during, immediately after, and after interventions. Pooled g was not retrieved — [Bournemouth record](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/38169/); [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/test2021/5166)
- Fincham et al. 2023 (*Sci Rep*) meta-analysed 12 RCTs (n = 785) of breathwork. Effect on self-reported stress was g = −0.35 (95% CI −0.55 to −0.14). Anxiety g = −0.32 and depression g = −0.40, from secondary analyses of 20 and 18 studies respectively. Most studies had moderate risk of bias — [PMC9828383](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/); [Sussex figshare](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Effect_of_breathwork_on_stress_and_mental_health_a_meta-analysis_of_randomised-controlled_trials/23494097/1)
- A later placebo-controlled RCT by Fincham and colleagues found no effect of coherent (slow) breathing beyond a well-designed breathwork placebo (search summary) — [Sci Rep s41598-023-49279-8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49279-8.pdf)
- Acute sport test: in a doctoral study, 36 adolescent male student-athletes completed a reactive stress-tolerance test after a single 20-min HRV-training session and after a control condition. HRVT **did not improve performance** under pressure — [Fountoulakis PhD thesis, MMU](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/625932/1/Charalampos%20FOUNTOULAKIS-18059857-%20PHD%20THESIS%20-%20FINAL%20210420.pdf)
- In a smartphone slow-paced breathing programme for dual-career athletes, athletes *reported* less pre-performance anxiety and more focus (self-report; design not confirmed) — [Bournemouth PDF](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/38363/14/A%20Smartphone%20Enabled%20Slow-Paced%20Breathing%20Intervention%20in%20Dual%20Career%20Athletes.pdf)
- A narrative review concludes the breathing–sports-performance connection "is still unclear," though slow breathing may help focus and concentration. Resonance-frequency protocols are usually multi-session — [PMC10224217](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10224217/)

**Cyclic sighing and box breathing**
- Balban et al. 2023 (*Cell Reports Medicine*; remote RCT; n = 108, mostly Stanford undergraduates; 5 min/day for 1 month). Arms were cyclic sighing, box breathing (equal inhale/hold/exhale), cyclic hyperventilation with retention, and mindfulness meditation. Cyclic sighing gave a significantly greater increase in daily positive affect and a greater reduction in respiratory rate than mindfulness. All groups improved affect. There was **no change in HRV, resting HR, sleep or trait anxiety** in any group, and benefits accumulated over days. Effect sizes were not retrieved — [PubMed 36630953](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/); [PMC9873947](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/breathwork-may-improve-mood-and-change-physiological-states-more-effectively-than-mindfulness-meditation/)
- A replication (University of Waterloo conference poster, 2024, N = 167) found cyclic sighing affected HRV and breathing rate but did **not** generalise to mood or anxiety. The authors suggest the original mood result may be a Type I error. This is a poster, not peer reviewed — [Waterloo poster PDF](https://uwaterloo.ca/psychology-discovery-conference/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/boucher_pdc_2024.pdf)
- I found no controlled study of box breathing as an *acute* pre-competition anxiety or performance intervention. Its use in sport rests on practice and anecdote — gap; see [Balban](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/) for the only RCT arm found

**Music**
- Terry, Karageorghis, Curran, Martin & Parsons-Smith 2020 (*Psychological Bulletin* 146(2):91–117) is a multilevel meta-analysis of 139 studies and 598 effect sizes. Results: affective valence g = 0.48 (95% CI 0.39–0.56), physical performance g = 0.31 (0.25–0.36), heart rate g = 0.07 (−0.03 to 0.16, n.s.). Effects were small overall. Performance effects were larger in exercise than in sport, and for fast vs. slow/medium tempo — [USQ record](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/q57x6/effects-of-music-in-exercise-and-sport-a-meta-analytic-review); [Brunel AAM](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/19875)
- Bishop, Karageorghis & Kinrade 2009 (n = 54 tennis players): faster pre-task tempo produced more pleasant and aroused states, and higher intensity (loudness) produced higher arousal and *faster choice reaction time*. The thesis and paper share one dataset — [Brunel 2438/3111](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/3111); [Brunel 2438/3667](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/3667)
- In Bishop, Wright & Karageorghis 2014 (fMRI), pre-task music tempo and intensity modulated neural activity during a subsequent reactive task — [Brunel 2438/9047](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/9047)
- A 2023 study frames pre-task music as having a residual "psych-up/psych-down" effect, possibly stronger for short anaerobic bouts — [SAGE 10.1177/20592043231174388](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20592043231174388)

### Inferences
- **Down-regulation (anxious players):** slow breathing with a long exhale is a reasonable, low-risk tool that measurably shifts physiology. App copy should promise "steadier body/breathing," not guaranteed calm or better performance. A few minutes of practice before the match is plausible, but the dose for acute anxiety reduction in athletes is unestablished.
- **Up-regulation (flat players):** fast, loud, liked music is the better-supported lever (affect g ≈ 0.5, performance g ≈ 0.3, but smaller in sport than exercise). Faster breathing or hyperventilation-style "activation" breathing for psych-up had no sport evidence in what I found.
- The music meta-analysis found no meaningful HR change, so the app shouldn't claim that music tempo "sets your heart rate."

### Gaps
- Pooled vmHRV effect sizes (Laborde 2022) and Balban effect sizes were not retrieved.
- No volleyball- or open-skill-specific acute breathing RCT was found.
- I found no study comparing slow breathing with music head-to-head for pre-match arousal regulation.

## 7. Choking, attentional control, self-focus and external focus

### Takeaway
Choking has two main accounts: self-focus/explicit monitoring/reinvestment (Beilock; Masters) and distraction. Both have support, and self-focus accounts fit well-learned sport skills. External focus (on the effect, ball or target) outperformed internal focus in Chua et al. 2021. A 2024 robust Bayesian re-analysis finds strong publication bias and near-zero corrected effects. **Grade: choking mechanisms Mixed–Strong; external-focus superiority Mixed (contested).**

### Cited Findings
- In the distraction account, attention goes to task-irrelevant cues such as worries or crowd noise. In the self-focus account, attention goes to step-by-step procedural control of movement. Reinvestment theory holds that people who consciously reinvest in automated movements control them explicitly and degrade them. The Movement-Specific Reinvestment Scale (Masters et al. 2005) measures this tendency — [Padua thesis](https://thesis.unipd.it/retrieve/e199e812-8dd8-43a9-8754-9a8df9ca0ac6/szarka_ivett.pdf)
- "Much support" exists for both self-focus accounts (Beilock & Gray 2007; Masters & Maxwell 2008). Beilock & Carr (2001) suggest both choking theories are relevant, with self-focus theories more applicable to sport — [Exeter manuscript](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/handle/10871/38553/Manuscript.pdf?sequence=2)
- A systematic review of conscious motor processing and the pressure–performance relationship covers the same area — [MMU e-space](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/630489/3/Conscious%20motor%20processing%20and%20the%20pressure-performance%20relationship-%20A%20systematic%20review%20%28minor%20revisions%29%20final%20version%20%28with%20tables%29.pdf)
- A 2017 review of choking interventions found benefit from interventions based on both models and from acclimatisation (practising under pressure) — [Int Rev Sport Exerc Psychol 10.1080/1750984X.2017.1408134](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1750984X.2017.1408134)
- **Chua, Jimenez-Diaz, Lewthwaite, Kim & Wulf 2021** (*Psychological Bulletin* 147(6):618–645) found external > internal focus for performance (g = 0.264), retention (g = 0.583) and transfer (g = 0.584) across age, health and skill level — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/superiority-of-external-attentional-focus-for-motor-performance-a/); [UNLV](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/424)
- **McKay et al. 2024** ("Reporting bias, not external focus," robust Bayesian meta-analysis) found moderate-to-strong evidence of publication bias in all five analyses. Corrected effects were negligible (e.g., g = .01 performance, g = .15 retention). They acknowledge that Chua et al. computed worst-case estimates — [preprint PDF (GitHub)](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2024_mckay-etal_reporting-bias-not-external-focus-a-robust-bayesian-meta-analysis-and-systematic-review-of-the-external-focus-of-attention.pdf); [SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/304/826)
- Flow neuroscience converges here: reduced self-referential/MPFC activity in flow (Ulrich), and reduced verbal-analytic influence in elite table-tennis players (Wolf, secondary) — [PubMed 23959200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23959200/); [PMC8205149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8205149)

### Inferences
- The app should avoid pre-match technique checklists ("elbow high, wrist snap") that invite reinvestment. Short, outcome- or target-oriented cues ("hit the seam," "ball to target") are low-risk. Copy should not claim a large proven boost given the McKay re-analysis.
- Practising under mild pressure (acclimatisation) has intervention support and may be worth more than breathing alone.

### Gaps
- No full-text check of Chua 2021 moderators (e.g., expert athletes, sport-specific tasks) was possible.
- No volleyball-specific choking or attentional-focus trial was found.
- Beilock's own primary experiments (e.g., Beilock & Carr 2001) were not retrieved directly; they are cited via secondary sources.
