# Eye-Hand Coordination and Visual-Motor Training for Athletes: Evidence for a Volleyball Pre-Match App

Note on method: 25 searches and attempted fetches, run 2026-11-23. WebFetch could not reach frontiersin.org, link.springer.com or pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov from this session, which blocks those hosts. So the findings below come from search-result abstracts and excerpts, not full-text reads. Effect sizes are reported only where a search excerpt stated them. Check them against the full text before quoting them in product copy.

Evidence-strength labels used: **STRONG** (several meta-analyses or RCTs agree), **MIXED** (some controlled evidence, but inconsistent, small or with no field transfer), **WEAK/MARKETING** (uncontrolled, single-team, vendor-driven or mechanistic only).

## 1. What do reviews and meta-analyses say about sports vision and perceptual-cognitive training, and about far transfer?

### Takeaway
Generic, decontextualised visual and cognitive training (light boards, multiple object tracking, app-style drills) reliably improves performance **on the trained task and close variants**. Evidence that it transfers to real sport performance is weak to mixed. Training that keeps sport-specific information in the stimulus, such as video occlusion of real opponents, has the best transfer evidence. Apparent gains in many digital-training RCTs are inflated when the test resembles the training task. **Overall: MIXED for sport-specific perceptual-cognitive training, WEAK for generic or digital "brain/vision" training.**

### Cited Findings
- Harris, Wilson & Vine (2018) systematically reviewed commercial cognitive-training devices for use in sport. They found limited support for far transfer to sporting tasks: 17 studies assessed transfer beyond lab cognitive tests, but **only 1 directly assessed transfer to a sporting task**. They concluded there is "little direct evidence" that these devices benefit sport performance. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00709/pdf); [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/35068?show=full); [PMC5958310](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5958310)
- Vater, Gray & Holcombe (2021) critically reviewed Neurotracker (3D multiple object tracking) across 29 articles. Only 3 studies tested far transfer to ecologically valid tasks, and 2 of those found no effect. There were no preregistered studies, the authors called evidence for working-memory and sustained-attention gains "very weak", and they said the abilities the tool trains are not the ones its makers advertise. — [Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01892-2); [PubMed 33821464](https://www.pubmed.gov/33821464/)
- Harris et al. (2020) tested NeuroTracker training and found no near transfer (to another object-tracking task) and no far transfer (to a real-world multitasking route-monitoring task). — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020 / PMC7028766](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7028766/)
- An ecological-dynamics critique, "Evaluating Weaknesses of 'Perceptual-Cognitive Training' and 'Brain Training' Methods in Sport", argues that generic training separates perception from action and from sport-specific information. — [PMC6348252](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6348252/)
- A 2024 *Sports Medicine* article, as summarised in search results, states "there is no supporting evidence for a far transfer of general perceptual or cognitive training to sports performance". I could not open the page to confirm its title and authors. — [Sports Medicine s40279-024-02060-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02060-x)
- Appelbaum & Erickson's "Sports vision training: A review of the state-of-the-art in digital training techniques" (Int Rev Sport Exerc Psychol 11(1):160–189; online Dec 2016, volume 2018) reviews analog drills through to digital tools. It rests on the premise that demanding visual, perceptual or oculomotor practice *may* improve processing and performance. I could not access its detailed conclusions. — [DukeSpace](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/00674222-b2d2-445e-b42a-babdf184939c)
- Hadlow et al. (2018) proposed the "Modified Perceptual Training Framework" (J Sci Med Sport 21(9):950–958). It predicts transfer from three interacting design factors: the **targeted perceptual function**, the **stimulus** (generic vs sport-specific), and the **response/tool** (button press vs sport-specific action). The more representative each factor is, the more likely transfer. — [VU Research Portal](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/modified-perceptual-training-in-sport-a-new-classification-framew/); [core.ac.uk PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/303683883.pdf)
- Zhu et al. (2024, *Behavioral Sciences* 14(10):919; 22 studies, 45 effect sizes, team sports) found that perceptual-cognitive training improved anticipation and decision-making, but "its transfer effects were limited". It was published in Behavioral Sciences, not Sports Medicine. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328x/14/10/919); [PMC11505547](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11505547/)
- Müller, Morris-Binelli, Hambrick & Macnamara (2024, *Sports Medicine*) meta-analysed 12 studies (25 effect sizes) of **video-based temporal occlusion training**. They found large improvements in anticipation that transferred to both lab and field tests, with no difference in benefit between video and field transfer tests. — [PMC11467115](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11467115/); [PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/s40279-024-02073-6.pdf)
- Guo, Yuan, Yang & Qiu (2025, *Frontiers in Physiology*; 33 RCTs, n=1,048; PROSPERO CRD420251020142) found that visual training improved visual attention, reaction time, decision time and accuracy, and eye-hand coordination. Studies with a likely **learning effect** (training and test tasks overlapping) showed much larger effects than those without: visual attention SMD **1.65 vs 0.07**, reaction time SMD **2.66 vs 0.50**. The authors recommend structurally distinct outcome measures and retention tests. — [PMC12446229](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12446229/); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1664572/full)
- A 2025 Frontiers review, per search summary, reports that decision-making gains from perceptual-cognitive training were larger in lab tests than in field assessments. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1697425/epub)
- A 2024 systematic review, "Role of Sport Vision in Performance", reported benefits but did not pool results in a meta-analysis because samples, techniques and treatments varied too much and some articles could not be evaluated. — [PubMed 38921628](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38921628/); [PMC11204951](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11204951/)
- A 2024 scoping review found 411 empirical papers on visual *assessment* but only 98 on vision-training *interventions*. — [PubMed 38815973](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38815973/)

### Inferences
- Under the Hadlow framework, all 4 current Flow of Ace drills are "generic stimulus + generic response (tap)". That is the class with the weakest transfer evidence.
- The Guo 2025 finding implies that any in-app "your reaction time improved X%" metric will mostly reflect task familiarity. It should not be marketed as on-court improvement.
- The strongest-evidence direction for a phone is **volleyball-specific video anticipation (temporal occlusion)**, not abstract dots.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the exact title and authors of the 2024 Sports Medicine commentary (s40279-024-02060-x), because the fetch was blocked.
- I could not read the Appelbaum & Erickson (2018) full conclusions or the Guo 2025 subgroup table.

## 2. Clark et al. (2012) Cincinnati baseball vision training, and critiques

### Takeaway
Clark et al. reported large batting gains after vision training, but the study was an uncontrolled, single-team before/after comparison confounded by an NCAA bat-rule change. A later, better-designed placebo-controlled RCT (Liu et al., 2020) found modest gains in batting practice but **no** improvement in game statistics or visual-motor measures. **Clark 2012: WEAK. Liu 2020: MIXED/preliminary.**

### Cited Findings
- Clark, Ellis, Bench, Khoury & Graman (2012, PLoS ONE 7(1):e29109) used Dynavision, Eyeport, a Bernell rotation trainer and strobe glasses, starting 6 weeks preseason with in-season maintenance. Team batting average rose from .251 (2010) to .285 (2011), and slugging rose by .033. — [PMC3261847](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3261847); [UC News](https://www.uc.edu/news/articles/legacy/healthnews/2012/01/enhanced-vision-training-boosts-batters-research-shows.html)
- Critique from the BPS Research Digest: no data on training-task changes, no analysis linking training changes to batting, and a concurrent NCAA bat-regulation change as a confound. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/could-sportsmen-and-women-benefit-vision-training)
- Liu et al. (2020, *Psychology of Sport and Exercise*) ran a pre-registered, randomized, placebo-controlled trial with 24 NCAA D1 batters. Batting-practice launch angle improved (d = 0.74) and hit distance improved (d = 0.70) versus placebo, but there were **no group differences in visual-motor skill measures or NCAA game statistics**. Many players had little game time, and the authors called the results preliminary. — [DukeSpace](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/dbcfe360-6227-40e9-ab4d-b5245353ea62/full); [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.02.12.945824v1.full.pdf); [Duke Today](https://today.duke.edu/2020/07/how-vision-training-can-turn-those-ground-balls-line-drives-0)

### Inferences
- Clark 2012 is widely used in vision-training marketing. The app should not cite it as proof of efficacy.

### Gaps
- No volleyball equivalent of Clark or Liu (whole-team vision-training effect on match statistics) was found.

## 3. Stroboscopic training, including volleyball

### Takeaway
Stroboscopic training has lab RCT support for some visual-cognitive gains, and small RCTs in volleyball and other sports report better visuomotor reaction and reactive agility. Recent meta-analyses are supportive but heterogeneous. It is the best-supported "vision training" device, but it works **during sport-specific drills**, not on a phone. **MIXED, leaning positive.**

### Cited Findings
- Appelbaum et al. (2011, Frontiers in Psychology): strobe training gave greater retest gains in central-field motion sensitivity and transient attention. There were **no** gains for peripheral motion sensitivity, peripheral transient attention, or sustained attention in multiple object tracking. — [Frontiers 2011](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00276/text)
- Appelbaum et al. (2012, Atten Percept Psychophys 74(8):1681–1691): strobe training improved short-term memory retention at longer delays (640–2,560 ms), and the benefit persisted 24 h later. — [DukeSpace](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/568d8558-2c2b-4048-ba38-bdcf3d3ad436); [DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.3758/s13414-012-0344-6)
- Wilkins & Appelbaum's review ("An early review of stroboscopic visual training", Int Rev Sport Exerc Psychol 13(1), online 2019, volume 2020) frames transfer to sport as *theorized*, not established. — [DukeSpace](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/3345ba0f-c39b-41cd-9627-50f1b773dfcd)
- Zwierko et al. (2023, PeerJ) randomized 50 youth volleyball players to 6 weeks of identical volleyball-specific drills with or without strobe glasses. The strobe group improved more in reactive agility and visuomotor function. Simple reaction time and sensory sensitivity did not change significantly. — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/15213); [PMC10211363](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10211363/)
- A 2024 BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil RCT (50 athletes, 25 per arm, 6 weeks) reported improved visuomotor reaction speed in agility-based, goal-directed movements. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s13102-024-00848-y)
- A Nov 2025 meta-analysis (9 articles, 323 athletes) on reaction time and decision-making found effects varied by sport type (open vs closed skill). — [PMC12665768](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12665768/)
- A Dec 2025 BMC systematic review found strobe training gave larger improvements than conventional training in hand-eye coordination (2 studies), reaction time (6), agility (1) and reactive agility (3). It called strobe training an "effective adjunct" and noted heterogeneous protocols. — [BMC s13102-025-01459-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-025-01459-x); see also [PMC12695576](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12695576/), [PubMed 41419922](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41419922/)
- Several volleyball strobe RCTs are registered but have no results yet: NCT07308535 (Senaptec glasses during passing, reception and blocking), ISRCTN10147263 (strobe plus reaction lights, collegiate men, 2026), and NCT07162545. — [NCT07308535](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07308535); [ISRCTN10147263](https://www.isrctn.com/pdf/ISRCTN10147263); [NCT07162545](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07162545)
- **Acute use:** adding strobe eyewear to the ball-specific phase of a soccer warm-up (n=22) improved reactive agility *with the ball* but not without it. The authors stress task specificity. — [Frontiers Physiology / digibug](https://digibug.ugr.es/handle/10481/97665)

### Inferences
- A phone cannot deliver real strobe training, because the athlete must move and play while vision is intermittently occluded. The app could *recommend* strobe glasses for team practice or simulate intermittent visibility in video clips. The second option is untested.

### Gaps
- No published RCT in volleyball reports transfer to match statistics. The registered trials are still pending.

## 4. Quiet eye (QE): meta-analysis, pressure training, volleyball

### Takeaway
Longer QE is reliably linked with expertise and successful trials, and QE training improves both QE and performance in aiming tasks. It also helps keep performance up under pressure (golf, basketball). Volleyball QE studies exist only for serve and serve reception in beginners, with tiny samples. **STRONG for closed aiming skills; WEAK/early for volleyball.** QE is a **pre-performance routine** skill (serve), not something trained by tapping dots.

### Cited Findings
- Lebeau et al. (2016, J Sport Exerc Psychol 38(5):441–457), meta-analysis:
  - Experts showed longer QE than novices (d = 1.04).
  - Successful trials showed longer QE than unsuccessful ones (d = 0.58).
  - Across 9 intervention articles, QE training increased QE duration (d = 1.53) and performance (d = 0.84).
  - QE changes partly predicted performance changes.

  — [PubMed 27633956](https://www.pubmed.gov/27633956/); [Lund repository](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/6ef0330b-9a48-4cc0-b8ea-83f98afc5b5a); [accepted MS](https://idus.us.es/handle/11441/53483)
- Vine & Wilson (2011, Acta Psychologica): QE training and pressure effects on attention and visuomotor control (basketball free throw). — [PubMed 21276584](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21276584/); [PMC3111367](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3111367)
- Elite golfers given QE training built into a pre-shot routine kept gaze control and accuracy under anxiety and made 1.9 fewer putts per round in competition. Controls did not change. — [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/9608); [Glos PDF](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/2870/1/Quiet%20eye%20training%20facilitates%20competitive%20putting%20performance.pdf)
- Anxiety reduces QE duration and free-throw success (Wilson, Vine & Wood 2009, n=10). — [Exeter PDF](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/handle/10871/9606/Vine%20S%20-%20Wilson%2C%20Vine%20%26%20Wood%20%282009%29.The%20Influence%20of%20Anxiety.pdf?sequence=2); [Exeter QE overview](https://medicine.exeter.ac.uk/phss/research/psychology/quieteye/)
- Volleyball QE:
  - Sharafian et al. (2019): 20 girls aged 10–12 practised forearm serve reception. The QE-training group improved more than the technical-training group at retention (P = 0.007), but QE duration did not differ between groups. — [Int J School Health](https://doaj.org/article/3953f233a1c04fdd94385dfe651999a4)
  - Zali et al. (2023): 18 beginner male students. QE training improved serve performance and QE duration. — [J Motor Control & Learning](https://brieflands.com/journals/jmcl/articles/144164)
  - Umezaki (Japan KAKEN report): QE training "may be effective" for judging spike course in reception among beginners. — [KAKEN PDF](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/ja/file/KAKENHI-PROJECT-16K16549/16K16549seika.pdf)
- I did not locate Rienhoff et al. (2016, review of QE mechanisms) or a Vickers volleyball-specific study in this search budget.

### Inferences
- The best fit for the app is a **serve pre-performance routine with QE cueing**: a final fixation on the target zone or ball held for about 1–2 s before the toss. It can be rehearsed off-screen in the warm-up and practised with a real ball. A phone can coach the routine with audio cues, but it cannot measure gaze reliably.

### Gaps
- No volleyball QE study in skilled or elite players with match outcomes was found.
- Rienhoff 2016 and the Vickers volleyball work were not retrieved.

## 5. Light-board reaction systems (Dynavision, BlazePod, FitLight)

### Takeaway
These devices are reasonably **reliable measurement tools after familiarisation**. Training studies show large gains on the device and on related reaction or agility tests, but controls often improve too, samples are small, and transfer to game performance is essentially untested. **MIXED for reaction and agility tests; WEAK for sport performance.**

### Cited Findings
- Dynavision D2 reliability (Wells et al., 2014, J Sports Sci Med; 42 recreationally active adults): fair to strong test-retest reliability, visual reaction time ICC 0.84. Reliable "when adequate practice is provided", with 1–3 familiarisation trials recommended. — [JSSM 13:145](https://jssm.org/jssm-13-145.xml-Fulltext)
- Wells & Johnson (2022, J Sport Rehabil): about **15 tests** are needed to get past the training effect and reach a stable baseline. Early gains are mostly task learning. — [utc.edu](https://utc.edu/document/7091)
- NCAA D1 football (n=62; poster abstract): visuomotor training cut D2 reaction time from about 773 to 548 ms. Reaction time of 765 ms or more was associated with roughly double the odds of sprains and strains. Durability is unknown. — [PMC6335314](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6335314)
- BlazePod response time showed moderate-to-excellent ICC test-retest reliability. — [PMC12411542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12411542)
- FitLight training in youth basketball (18 weeks, n=70) gave large gains in reaction time and spatial orientation. — [PMC9695455](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9695455/). A 2025 study in female basketball players also showed control-group improvement. — [Frontiers Sports 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1623558/full)
- A FitLight reliability and acute-effects trial is registered, with no results found. — [NCT06748417](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06748417)

### Inferences
- Light pods are "physical saccade-jump tapping" with whole-body movement and a real reach. They sit nearer the action than a phone screen but are still generic stimuli. A cheap substitute is a partner pointing or calling colours.

### Gaps
- No volleyball-specific light-pod RCT with on-court outcomes was found.

## 6. Volleyball visual skills: gaze in reception, blocking and setting; occlusion and video training

### Takeaway
Expert volleyball players differ mainly in **where and when** they look (setter body and hands, use of visual pivots, parafoveal pick-up), not in basic visual acuity or reaction speed. Generic off-court MOT training in elite volleyball players showed **no far transfer** to a volleyball blocking test. Video-occlusion training has good cross-sport transfer evidence, but I found no volleyball-specific occlusion RCT with on-court transfer.

### Cited Findings
- Vansteenkiste et al. (2014, Biology of Sport 31(4):295–302; 37 women, elite/intermediate/novice) found broadly similar gaze across groups with "visual pivots" at reception and final pass. Experts extract more parafoveal information and look more at the setter. — [PMC4296842](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4296842); [Termedia](https://termedia.pl/CUE-USAGE-IN-VOLLEYBALL-A-TIME-COURSE-COMPARISON-OF-ELITE-INTERMEDIATE-AND-NOVICE-FEMALE-PLAYERS,78,24333,0,0.html)
- Piras, Lobietti & Squatrito: in video setter sequences, experts showed more consistent gaze strategies and anticipated set direction, and experienced players watch the early ball flight and then the setter. — [J Ophthalmology 2014 / PMC4021845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4021845); [Unibo](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/389520)
- Scientific Reports (2020): just before ball contact, the opposing players' upper body is the most relevant attentional cue in all game situations (setters vs other experts). — [Nature Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-74487-x.pdf); [PMC7560879](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7560879)
- Blockers predicted set direction more accurately when gaze was on the ball and the setter's wrists rather than the setter's head (video eye-tracking study, cited in a 2024 paper). — [PMC10968438](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10968438)
- Fleddermann, Heppe & Zentgraf (2019; 22 elite volleyball players, 8 weeks of 3D-MOT, vs 21 controls) found large task-specific gains (ηp² = 0.70) and two near-transfer gains, but **no** far transfer to a volleyball blocking test. — [PMC6667634](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6667634/); [PubMed 31396123](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31396123/)
- Cross-sport occlusion meta-analysis: large anticipation gains with transfer to field tests (see Q1). — [PMC11467115](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11467115/)
- In cricket, combining occlusion training with physical practice of the observed action improved transfer. — [Brunel PDF](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/14345/1/FullText.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Directly relevant:** Flow of Ace's "multiple object tracking 3-of-8" drill is essentially the Fleddermann intervention, which failed to transfer to a volleyball task even with 8 weeks of training in elite players. It is therefore unlikely to do anything on-court as a pre-match drill.
- A better phone drill: short clips of a setter or attacker (first-person, from the receiver or blocker view), occluded at contact. The player predicts set direction or attack zone with a swipe, then gets feedback, and cue prompts can direct gaze to the setter's hands or the attacker's shoulder. This matches the Hadlow framework on stimulus and is the class with meta-analytic transfer support.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Afonso et al.'s volleyball gaze and decision-making work.
- No volleyball occlusion RCT was found. The app would be extrapolating from tennis, cricket and soccer.

## 7. Simple physical drills: wall catches, reaction balls, juggling, catch-throw

### Takeaway
There is little controlled sport-transfer evidence for any single simple drill. Juggling shows training-induced brain plasticity in visual-motion areas, but that is mechanistic evidence, not performance transfer. Physical drills do respect the perception-action coupling that phone tapping lacks. **WEAK (mechanistic or plausibility) for specific simple drills.**

### Cited Findings
- Draganski et al. (2004, Nature 427:311–312): 3 months of three-ball cascade juggling transiently increased grey matter in hMT/V5 (visual motion) and the left posterior intraparietal sulcus. The increase receded after juggling stopped and was linked to juggling performance. — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/427311a.pdf); [UW summary](https://staff.washington.edu/chudler/jugg.html)
- The ecological-dynamics critique argues that perception and action must stay coupled for transfer, which favours ball-based and movement-based drills over button-pressing. — [PMC6348252](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6348252/)
- The strongest physical-drill evidence is for strobe glasses **added to sport drills** (see Q3), and for acute strobe use in the ball phase of a warm-up (see Q3).

### Gaps
- I found no RCTs of reaction balls, wall-ball catching or tennis-ball partner drills with sport outcomes, so their effects are not established. Their low cost and low risk, plus the fact that the response is a real catch, make them sensible warm-up content. That last point is an inference, not a finding.

## 8. Phone and tablet touch-screen training: athlete studies and limits

### Takeaway
I found no RCT showing that phone or tablet visual drills improve on-court performance. Touchscreens add 20–200 ms of device-dependent latency, which is similar in size to the reaction-time differences being "trained". Phones also offer a small field of view and a tap response that does not resemble sport movement. **WEAK for phone-based eye-hand training as performance enhancement.**

### Cited Findings
- Smartphone touch-device latencies range from about 50 to 200 ms, against human simple response times of 200–300 ms. This was measured robotically across 26 phones. — [PubMed 35953659](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35953659/)
- Tablet timing errors were 36–105 ms depending on device and browser (Samsung tablet worst at 88–105 ms). The authors concluded touchscreens are "not well suited" to simple reaction-time collection. — [PMC9918597](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9918597)
- The closest athlete touchscreen study is Schorer et al.'s soccer pattern-recall study on a 23-inch touchscreen. It found some benefit on computerised retention tests, but there was no field test and no placebo. — [PMC6115512](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6115512)
- Guo 2025: training-test similarity greatly inflates digital visual-training effects (see Q1). — [PMC12446229](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12446229/)

### Inferences
- **Saccade-jump dot tapping:** trains saccade-and-tap on a 6-inch field. In volleyball the visual field spans tens of degrees and the response is a whole-body movement. Transfer is unlikely. Reaction-time scores will be confounded by device latency, so cross-device leaderboards are invalid.
- **UFOV peripheral flash:** UFOV training literature comes mainly from older adults and driving, and none of it is in athletes (not searched in depth). A phone held at arm's length covers only about 10–15° of visual angle (my estimate, not sourced), so it barely engages true periphery. Appelbaum 2011 also found no peripheral gains even from strobe training.
- **MOT 3-of-8:** directly contradicted by Fleddermann 2019 (no volleyball far transfer).
- **Coincidence-anticipation timing:** the most volleyball-relevant of the four, because ball-arrival timing matters. But I found no evidence that Bassin-style CAT training transfers to interception. — [Sport Journal pilot](https://thesportjournal.org/article/coincidence-anticipation-timing-requirements-across-different-stimulus-speeds-in-various-sports-a-pilot-study/)
- Phone strengths that are realistic: delivering **video-based anticipation clips**, cueing a **QE serve routine**, guiding a **physical warm-up** (partner and ball drills, timers), and *measuring* within-person trends on the same device.

### Gaps
- No athlete RCT on a smartphone or tablet visual-motor app with field outcomes was found.
- UFOV-in-athletes evidence was not searched in depth.

## 9. Acute effects: does a short visual warm-up help, and can pre-match screen or cognitive tasks cause mental fatigue?

### Takeaway
I found no good evidence that a few minutes of generic visual drills right before competition improves match performance. Only task-specific acute strobe use in a ball warm-up shows an acute benefit. Conversely, **about 30 min of smartphone use before play can induce mental fatigue and impair decision-making in volleyball**, though findings are mixed. Mental fatigue reliably harms skilled performance in meta-analyses. **Acute visual warm-up benefit: WEAK. Risk of pre-match screen-induced mental fatigue: MIXED but credible.**

### Cited Findings
- Acute strobe in the ball-specific soccer warm-up improved reactive agility with the ball only (n=22). — [digibug](https://digibug.ugr.es/handle/10481/97665)
- An intense anaerobic bout may transiently enhance visual performance (Essex study; a 2025 study in inactive adults). The effect in athletes is unknown. — [Essex PDF](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/34712/1/Effects%20of%20a%20maximal%20cycling%20all%20out%20anaerobic%20test%20on%20visual%20performance.pdf); [Frontiers Physiol 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1583286/full)
- Fortes et al. (2021): 4 weeks of 30 min of social media before training (n=12 vs 12 young volleyball players). Only the control group improved attack and passing decision-making. — [PubMed 34000894](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34404292/); [Unibo PDF](https://cris.unibo.it/bitstream/11585/945196/6/HAPN-2021-0034.R2_Proof_hi.pdf)
- A randomized crossover trial in young male volleyball players found pre-training smartphone social media caused mental fatigue and reduced attack efficiency. — [PMC11845908](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11845908/)
- A volleyball study of social-media mental fatigue on decision-making, endurance and countermovement jump found decision-making affected but jump unaffected. — [Unibo PDF](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/413e7e75-4050-4b25-b502-e65374281017/Effects%20of%20Mental%20Fatigue%20Induced%20by%20Social%20Media%20Use%20on%20Volleyball%20Decision-Making%2c%20Endurance%2c%20and%20Countermovement%20Jump%20Performance.pdf)
- **Contrary evidence:** in beach volleyball (2024), 30 min of social media raised subjective mental fatigue but attack decision-making was unchanged. — [Int J Sport Exerc Psychol 2024](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/1612197X.2024.2325585)
- A *Sports Medicine* 2021 systematic review of mental fatigue and sport-specific skilled performance reported impaired decision-making, reaction time and accuracy. I could not verify the author list (likely Sun et al.). — [DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01429-6](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S40279-021-01429-6)
- A soccer meta-analysis found mental fatigue impairs technical performance. — [PMC9354787](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9354787/)
- A bias-sensitive 2020 meta-analysis found small-to-medium effects on exercise performance and inconsistency across studies. — [PMC7546119](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7546119)

### Inferences
- These studies used 30+ min of social media. A 2–4 min app drill is far shorter and probably too brief to cause meaningful fatigue, but no study tests short cognitive drills right before matches. Keep pre-match screen time short (under 5 min) and end with eyes-off-screen, ball-in-hand activity.
- Any "pre-match" value of the 4 drills is likely **arousal and attentional priming or routine** (psychological readiness), not trained visual skill. Frame them that way.

### Gaps
- No study found tests a short (under 5 min) visual or cognitive screen warm-up immediately before a volleyball match.
- Smith et al.'s mental-fatigue work was not individually retrieved. The meta-analyses above cover the area.
