# Build flow conditions, retire the screen drills

The research supports a pre-match app that **raises the odds of flow and sharpens mental readiness**. It does not support an app that "triggers flow" or trains eye-hand coordination on a phone screen. The flow literature, from Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow* (1990) and Jackson & Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow in Sports* (1999) through Swann's systematic reviews, agrees on the conditions that make flow more likely: challenge matched to skill, clear goals, clear feedback, preparation, confidence, the right focus and the right level of arousal. A 2023 systematic review still found **no study showing that an intervention conclusively induced flow**. The most useful tools for a 5–15 minute window are acute mindset techniques that have controlled evidence: process goals, arousal reappraisal, challenge-framed instructions, if-then plans and trained pre-performance routines. Individually their effects range from small to moderate (d ≈ 0.2–0.65 in most meta-analyses). The eye-hand evidence points the other way for Flow of Ace's current visual drills. Generic tap-the-stimulus training improves the trained task but rarely transfers to sport. Multiple object tracking was tested directly in elite volleyball players and **did not transfer to a volleyball blocking test**. The phone-deliverable options with transfer evidence are **volleyball video-occlusion anticipation** (mainly as off-day training) and coaching a **quiet-eye serve routine** that the player then does with a real ball. The practical recommendation is a pre-match flow of roughly 10–12 minutes with under 5 minutes on screen: arousal tuning, a mindset set, a brief rehearsal of imagery and self-talk the player has already trained, and a short pair or team ritual, ending in a physical ball warm-up. A trained 3-second reset covers the points between rallies. Retire multiple object tracking and peripheral flash, demote dot tapping, and rebuild coincidence-anticipation timing around real volleyball video.

## Flow can be made more likely, never switched on

Csikszentmihalyi describes flow as total absorption in a task that is rewarding in itself. The book's central argument is that flow can be **cultivated rather than left to chance**, mainly by matching tasks to ability ([World Database of Happiness](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/flow-the-psychology-of-optimal-experience-441)). The standard framework has nine dimensions: challenge–skill balance, merging of action and awareness, clear goals, unambiguous feedback, concentration on the task, sense of control, loss of self-consciousness, transformation of time, and autotelic (intrinsically rewarding) experience ([PMC5973526](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5973526)). *Flow in Sports* applies the same nine to athletes and remains one of the field's main reference texts ([Leeds Beckett review](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/4768/1/AReviewofScientificProgressinFlowAM-PIGGOTT.pdf)). Harris et al. treat **three dimensions as preconditions you can design for**: challenge–skill balance, clear goals and feedback. The other six describe the experience itself ([Harris et al. 2021](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_the_relationship_between_flow_states_and_performance/29784977)). That split matters for product design, but it "was never tested in a theoretical model" ([PMC9022035](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9022035)), and a 2024 paper says flow theory has reached a "crisis point" ([SAGE 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09593543241237492)). The design consequence is to target the preconditions before the match and to measure the six experiential dimensions afterwards. Telling a player to "lose self-consciousness" is likely to backfire.

Jackson's interviews with elite athletes give a practical list. Her 1995 study coded 361 raw themes from **28 elite athletes in 7 sports**. The most prominent facilitators were **preparation, confidence, focus, how the performance felt and progressed, and optimal motivation and arousal**. Athletes saw most facilitators and preventers as controllable and saw disruptors as "largely … uncontrollable" ([Jackson 1995](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413209508406962)). Her figure-skater study added **unity with partner** for pairs skaters, which maps directly onto beach volleyball pairs ([Jackson 1992](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/10413209208406459)). Swann's 2012 review of 17 studies found flow "controllable to some extent rather than purely coincidental" ([Leeds Beckett](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/273)). The product therefore has two jobs. It should prepare the controllable facilitators, and it should rehearse a fast recovery from the uncontrollable disruptors: errors, calls and crowd noise.

Swann's later work changes what "peak state" should mean in the app. Excellent performances come from **two distinct states**. Flow ("letting it happen") tends to arise with open, exploratory goals. Clutch ("making it happen") is effortful and highly aware, and it arises with fixed, high-stakes goals. Athletes move between the two within a single performance ([Swann et al. 2017](https://ro.uow.edu.au/sspapers/2917); [Swann, Crust & Vella 2017](https://ro.uow.edu.au/sspapers/3024)). Volleyball has both. Long open rallies suit flow, while serving at 23–24 calls for clutch. The app should offer an open goal ("see how many aggressive serves I can land") alongside a "make it happen" cue for pressure points.

The case for caring about flow is real but moderate. The best meta-analysis finds **r = .31 (95% CI .24–.38)** between flow and performance across 22 studies ([Harris et al. 2021](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1929402)). That is about 10% of performance variance, and the direction of causality is unknown. The intervention record is weaker than the popular literature implies. A 2017 review of flow training in elite athletes found only six pre-experimental single-subject studies and one experiment ([Norsworthy et al. 2017](https://www2.worc.ac.uk/gjseper/documents/Flow_states_and_performance_in_elite_athletes_6_16-28.pdf)). The 2023 review found interventions "largely … unsuccessful at producing flow experiences" ([SCU record](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-systematic-review-of-flow-interventions/991012942199702368)). Mindfulness has the largest flow effect, **SMD 0.92**, but every program behind it ran **4–8 weeks**, and Noetel's review rated the evidence quality low ([Si et al. 2024](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1375608/full); [Noetel et al.](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1750984X.2017.1387803)). Imagery scripts built around the flow preconditions raised flow in 3 of 4 nationally ranked tennis players in a single-case design ([Koehn et al. 2014](https://hira.hope.ac.uk/id/eprint/1576/)).

Kotler's "flow triggers" (17, now cited as 22) and the "flow cycle" are useful vocabulary, but most triggers repackage Csikszentmihalyi's preconditions. His peer-reviewed paper calls itself a "proposal" ([Kotler et al. 2022](https://stevenkotler.com/papers/neuroscience-and-biobehavioral-review-2022)), and no peer-reviewed test of the cycle was found. The neuroscience does not support the popular claims either. "Transient hypofrontality" has **equivocal** imaging support ([Harris, Vine & Wilson 2017](https://www.pubmed.gov/29031465/)). Flow studies show *decreased* medial prefrontal and amygdala activity alongside *increased* inferior frontal activity ([Ulrich et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23959200/)). A 2022 review of 25 studies concluded that the neural correlates of flow cannot yet be determined ([Alameda et al. 2022](https://digibug.ugr.es/handle/10481/78493)). No study has measured dopamine or anandamide during verified flow in humans ([PMC12827708](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827708/)). Defensible copy is "absorbed, less self-talk, less sense of threat." "Brain off" and "dopamine flood" are not defensible.

For self-tracking, the validated instrument is the **9-item Short Flow State Scale-2**, one item per dimension, validated in samples of up to 865 ([Jackson, Martin & Eklund 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18971512/)). It is copyrighted, so the app either needs a licence or should use its own item wording without presenting it as the validated scale. Flow should be measured after the match, never during it.

## Minutes-before mindset tools carry the best acute evidence

Setting mindset is the area where an app has the strongest case, because several techniques were tested as **instructions delivered minutes before performance**. *Arousal reappraisal* means telling yourself "I'm excited" instead of "calm down". Brooks showed it improved singing, speaking and maths ([Brooks 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24364682/)). A 2024 meta-analysis of RCTs pooled **d = 0.23 overall and d = 0.34 for public performance tasks**, and called these interventions "not silver bullets" but low-cost ([Bosshard & Gomez 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10994935/)). *Challenge-state instructions* that stress resources and control, given just before putting, produced more accurate putts, better quiet-eye gaze and better kinematics in 127 novices ([Moore et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3677799)). They did the same for experienced golfers under competition ([Moore et al. 2013](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/2878/1/Champ%20or%20Chump.pdf)). An updated 2025 meta-analysis confirms small advantages for challenge over threat states ([Hase et al. 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11869992/)). *Process goals* show **d = 1.36 for performance versus d = 0.09 for outcome goals** ([Williamson et al. 2022](https://tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2022.2116723)), although the estimate rests on relatively few studies. *Implementation intentions* (if-then plans) average **d = 0.65** across 94 general studies ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/eb48c8cf-7855-478e-90ec-1ea6d44f8252/content)), and a tennis study showed they helped players manage disruptive states during matches ([Achtziger et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18272806/)). Each of these takes minutes to set up. Combined into one package they are untested, though mixed reappraisal interventions (k = 5) showed a larger d = 0.45.

Confidence is the strongest correlate of the bunch. Self-confidence predicts performance (**r = 0.24**) far better than cognitive anxiety does (**r = −0.10**) ([Woodman & Hardy 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12846532/)), and pre-event self-efficacy correlates at r = 0.31 ([Lochbaum et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10675036/)). No trial was found of a "highlight recall" card, so it is a plausible feature rather than a proven one. Gratitude, values and identity priming had **no evidence found** for pre-competition readiness.

The arousal evidence argues against a single "calm down" target. The IZOF meta-analysis (19 studies, 6,387 participants) supports **individual optimal zones** ([Jokela & Hanin 1999](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstreams/306dfd29-45aa-4514-a2ea-5ce9e3993c15/download)). Lab psychophysiology places flow at **moderate** sympathetic and cortisol arousal on an inverted U ([Peifer et al. 2014](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/the-relation-of-flow-experience-and-physiological-arousal-under-s/)). Catastrophe research shows hysteresis: once performance collapses under high anxiety, a small reduction in arousal does not restore it ([Bangor review](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/files/20572479/null)). Down-regulation should therefore aim at "steady and ready", not "sleepy", and should happen before the player tips over. The tools differ in strength. Slow breathing reliably raises vagal heart-rate variability ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/38169/)) and reduces self-reported stress modestly (**g = −0.35**) ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/)). However, a placebo-controlled trial found no advantage over sham breathwork ([Sci Rep 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49279-8.pdf)), and a single 20-minute HRV session **did not improve athletes' performance under pressure** ([Fountoulakis thesis](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/625932/1/Charalampos%20FOUNTOULAKIS-18059857-%20PHD%20THESIS%20-%20FINAL%20210420.pdf)). Cyclic sighing beat mindfulness on mood in one month-long trial ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)), but a replication poster failed on mood ([Waterloo poster](https://uwaterloo.ca/psychology-discovery-conference/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/boucher_pdc_2024.pdf)). No controlled acute test of box breathing in sport was found. For up-regulation, music is the better lever: **affect g = 0.48, performance g = 0.31**, larger with fast tempo, and **no meaningful heart-rate effect (g = 0.07)** ([Terry et al. 2020](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/q57x6/effects-of-music-in-exercise-and-sport-a-meta-analytic-review)). Fast, loud pre-task music sped choice reaction time in tennis players ([Bishop et al. 2009](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/3111)). No sport evidence was found for "activation" hyperventilation breathing.

Trained routines cover the seconds between points. Pre-performance routines improve performance (**SMC = 0.31** pre-post, moderate-to-large in experiments, including under pressure), and **routine type and skill level did not moderate the effect**, so players can personalize their routines ([Rupprecht et al. 2021](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1944271)). Quiet-eye training raises performance in aiming skills by **d = 0.84** ([Lebeau et al. 2016](https://www.pubmed.gov/27633956/)), and quiet-eye pre-shot routines protected elite golfers' putting under anxiety ([Exeter](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/9608)). Self-talk has **ES = 0.48**, works better when trained beforehand, and works best for fine or precision tasks ([Hatzigeorgiadis et al. 2011](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691611413136/)). Imagery has **d = 0.43**, with larger effects at higher training intensity ([Simonsmeier et al.](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/2943784)). Its best-dose estimate is about **10 minutes, 3 times a week, for about 100 days** ([2025 meta-analysis](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/5/685/html)). One caution applies across all of these. A 2024 meta-analysis found that the moderate effects of mindfulness, PST and imagery **lost significance once non-randomized trials and subjective outcomes were excluded** ([Sports Med 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37812334/)). Volleyball-specific evidence is thin: correlational serve-routine and imagery data from small NCAA squads ([Shoenfelt & Griffith 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18986056/)), and a 24-session study in novices ([Nazari 2024](https://brieflands.com/journals/jmcl/articles/144891)). No peer-reviewed controlled trial of between-point routines in tennis or volleyball was found.

Cue content matters because of choking. Self-focus and reinvestment accounts hold that **step-by-step technique monitoring degrades automated skills** ([Exeter manuscript](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/handle/10871/38553/Manuscript.pdf?sequence=2)). Pre-match cues should therefore be short and target-oriented ("ball to the seam"), not technique checklists ("elbow high, wrist snap"). External focus beat internal focus in a large meta-analysis (g = 0.26 for performance) ([Chua et al. 2021](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/superiority-of-external-attentional-focus-for-motor-performance-a/)). A 2024 bias-corrected re-analysis, however, put the corrected performance effect near **g = .01** ([McKay et al. 2024](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/304/826)). External cues are low-risk and sensible, but the app should not advertise them as a proven boost.

## Generic screen drills train the screen, not the court

The eye-hand evidence follows one rule. **The more a drill's stimulus and response resemble the sport, the more likely it transfers.** This is the Modified Perceptual Training Framework ([Hadlow et al. 2018](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/modified-perceptual-training-in-sport-a-new-classification-framew/)). Flow of Ace's four visual drills all combine a generic stimulus with a tap response, which is the weakest class. A review of commercial cognitive-training devices found that **only 1 of 17 transfer studies tested an actual sporting task** ([Harris, Wilson & Vine 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00709/pdf)). A review of 29 Neurotracker papers found 3 ecological far-transfer tests, 2 of them null, and no preregistered studies ([Vater et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01892-2)). A 2025 meta-analysis of 33 RCTs explains why marketing numbers look impressive. When training and test overlapped, visual-attention gains were **SMD 1.65; without overlap they were 0.07** ([Guo et al. 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12446229/)). Any in-app "reaction time improved X%" figure mostly measures familiarity with the task. Phones make this worse. Touch latency of **about 50–200 ms** across 26 phones is the same order as the differences being trained ([PubMed 35953659](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35953659/)), so cross-device leaderboards are invalid.

The most damaging single result for Flow of Ace is volleyball-specific. **Eight weeks of 3D multiple object tracking in 22 elite volleyball players produced large on-task gains (ηp² = 0.70) but no transfer to a volleyball blocking test** ([Fleddermann et al. 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6667634/)). That is close to the app's "3-of-8" drill, and it failed with a far larger dose than a pre-match session could deliver. Peripheral training fares little better. Even strobe training produced **no peripheral motion or peripheral attention gains** ([Appelbaum et al. 2011](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00276/text)), and a handheld screen barely reaches the periphery. No evidence was found that coincidence-anticipation timing training transfers to interception ([Sport Journal pilot](https://thesportjournal.org/article/coincidence-anticipation-timing-requirements-across-different-stimulus-speeds-in-various-sports-a-pilot-study/)). The much-cited Cincinnati baseball study was an uncontrolled single-team comparison confounded by a bat-rule change ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/could-sportsmen-and-women-benefit-vision-training)). The pre-registered placebo-controlled follow-up found **no gains in game statistics** ([Liu et al. 2020](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/dbcfe360-6227-40e9-ab4d-b5245353ea62/full)).

Three directions have better support. First, **video-based temporal occlusion training**, where the player predicts the outcome from a clip cut at contact, produced large anticipation gains that **transferred to field tests as well as lab tests** across 12 studies ([Müller et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11467115/)). This is the best phone-deliverable visual skill. Volleyball gaze research supplies the cues: experts read the setter's body and hands and use parafoveal vision, and they do not simply react faster ([Vansteenkiste et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4296842)). Blockers predict set direction better when they watch the ball and the setter's wrists ([PMC10968438](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10968438)). No volleyball occlusion RCT exists yet, so the app would be extrapolating from tennis, cricket and soccer. Second, the **quiet-eye serve routine** (Section 2) is the best-supported eye-hand skill for the serve, though volleyball studies so far involve only small samples of beginners ([Zali et al. 2023](https://brieflands.com/journals/jmcl/articles/144164)). Third, **stroboscopic glasses during real volleyball drills** improved reactive agility and visuomotor function in a 50-player youth volleyball RCT ([Zwierko et al. 2023](https://peerj.com/articles/15213)). That works on court, not on a phone, so it belongs in a coach feature. Simple physical drills (partner catches, reaction balls, juggling) have no sport-transfer RCTs. They keep perception and action coupled, though ([PMC6348252](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6348252/)), and they are cheap and low-risk.

Screen time itself carries a cost. **About 30 minutes of pre-training smartphone use induced mental fatigue and impaired attack efficiency or decision-making in young volleyball players** ([randomized crossover, PMC11845908](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11845908/); [Fortes et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34404292/)), although a beach-volleyball study found decisions unchanged ([2024](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/1612197X.2024.2325585)). A short app session is unlikely to fatigue players, and no study tests one. Even so, the right design keeps screen time short and ends with eyes off the screen and a ball in hand. Whatever pre-match value the tap drills have is arousal and attentional priming, not trained visual skill.

## Evidence ledger: what is strong, mixed or merely popular

The three tiers below separate what an app can claim from what it should only offer as a habit. "Acute" means the technique was tested as a single session or as instructions given minutes before the task. "Trained" means the evidence comes from multi-week programs.

## A 12-minute pre-match flow, a 3-second reset, and nine drill verdicts

### Recommended app flow

The sequence below is assembled from separately tested components. **The package as a whole is untested** and should be validated in the app itself, for example by tracking serve percentage against post-match flow ratings. It scales from 5 to 15 minutes by trimming or extending steps 2, 4 and 7. Screen time stays under about 5 minutes.

| Step | Time | Content | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Lock in | 20 s | Do Not Disturb prompt; one-tap state check: flat / ready / wired | Concentration precondition; IZOF personalization |
| 2. Arousal tuning (branched) | 2–3 min | **Wired:** slow breathing with long exhale, target "steady," plus a reappraisal line ("racing heart = ready; say *I'm excited*"). **Flat:** fast, loud, self-chosen music with up-tempo movement. **Ready:** short, liked track | Fincham; Laborde; Terry; Bosshard & Gomez |
| 3. Mindset set | 2 min | Challenge framing (resources, control); 1–3 process goals including one open goal; 2 if-then plans ("If I shank a pass, then exhale, 'next ball', call it early") | Moore; Williamson; Gollwitzer; Swann open goals |
| 4. Confidence + imagery | 60–90 s | Recall 2 past successes; run already-trained serve and pass imagery with external, target cues | Lochbaum; Simonsmeier; Koehn |
| 5. Pair or team unity | 30–60 s | Shared collective goal; partner ritual (beach pairs) | Jackson 1992; van den Hout team flow |
| 6. Anticipation primer (optional) | 1–2 min | 6–10 volleyball occlusion clips (setter hands, attacker shoulder), only if content exists | Müller 2024 (mainly an off-day tool) |
| 7. Eyes-off, ball-in-hand | 2–4 min | Audio-guided partner catches, reaction-ball or wall-toss, then 3–5 serve-routine rehearsals with quiet-eye fixation on the target zone | Ecological rationale; Lebeau; Rupprecht |

**Between points**, the 3-second reset is a trained routine: physical release and exhale, then a cue word, then the next process goal or target. Before a serve it extends to a quiet-eye fixation on the target. As an inference from the aiming literature, that fixation is about 1–2 s before the toss. A separate "make it happen" clutch cue covers set-point moments. **After the match**, the app asks a 3-item pulse (absorption, control, enjoyment) or a 9-item flow check-in in its own wording, plus a performance self-rating. Over time this lets the app learn each player's arousal zone. **On off-days**, it runs the trained components that cannot be crammed before a match: a 6–8-week mindfulness program ([Aherne 2011](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/the-effect-of-mindfulness-training-on-athletes-flow-an-initial-in/); [Scott-Hamilton 2016](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/18914)), imagery at about 10 min three times a week, self-talk cue training (10 weeks in the swimming study, [Hatzigeorgiadis 2014](https://eclass.uth.gr/modules/document/file.php/PE_P_160/self_talk/4.3.22Hatzigeorgiadis_etaL_2014.pdf)), and occlusion clip blocks. Pre-match versions of these only work as cues for skills the player has already trained.

### Drill-by-drill verdicts for the current app

| Current drill | Verdict | Evidence grade | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breathing + music arousal tuning | **Keep, change** | Breathing: strong for physiology, mixed for anxiety, weak for acute performance. Music: strong for affect, small for performance | Make it two-way and per-player. Use breathing to reach "steady," not maximal calm, and music for up-regulation. Drop any claim that tempo "sets heart rate" or releases neurochemicals. Log state against outcome to learn each player's zone. |
| Saccade dot tapping | **Demote or drop** | Weak; learning-effect and touch-latency confounds | At most a 20–30 s optional "wake-up" primer. No skill claims, no reaction-time scores across devices. |
| Peripheral flash | **Drop** | Weak; phone barely reaches the periphery; no peripheral gains even from strobe | Replace with a partner drill: call the colour or the number of fingers in your periphery while passing. |
| Multiple object tracking 3-of-8 | **Drop** | Directly tested and null in elite volleyball | Replace with volleyball occlusion clips. |
| Coincidence-anticipation timing | **Change** | Weak; no CAT transfer evidence | Rebuild as ball-arrival and direction prediction on real serve, set and spike video, occluded at contact, with gaze cues. Use mainly off-day. |
| Position visualization | **Keep, change** | Strong for performance (trained); mixed for flow | Script it around flow preconditions (goals, control, challenge) and external cues. Pre-match: a 60–90 s run-through. Build the trained dose (~10 min, 3x/wk) off-day. |
| Self-talk cue | **Keep, change** | Strong (best when trained) | Train cues in practice first. Use short instructional or target cues for the serve and other precision skills, and avoid technique checklists that invite reinvestment. |
| Activation card deck | **Change** | Depends on content; components range from strong to unevidenced | Restock with reappraisal, challenge-framing, process-goal, if-then and confidence-recall cards. Remove unevidenced cards: "trigger flow," neurochemistry, and gratitude or identity priming presented as performance aids. |
| 3-second reset routine | **Keep, change** | Strong for PPRs generally; between-point routines in volleyball untested | Fix the structure as release, then cue, then next process goal. Add a quiet-eye serve variant and a clutch variant. Rehearse it in practice so it runs automatically in matches. |

## Conclusion

The evidence supports a different job for Flow of Ace than its current drill list implies. Its strongest asset is not visual training. It is an acute **readiness protocol**: per-player arousal tuning, reappraisal, challenge framing, process and open goals, if-then plans and a trained reset. These tools have controlled, minutes-before evidence, and they act on the controllable facilitators that Jackson's athletes named. Most of the flow-specific gains (mindfulness, imagery, self-talk) build over weeks, so the app is likely to deliver more value as an **off-day trainer that pre-match cues draw on** than as a pre-match session alone. The flow–clutch distinction adds a volleyball-specific opportunity that competitors miss: coaching athletes to "let it happen" in open rallies and "make it happen" at 23–24.

The eye-hand findings point to a choice. If eye-hand work stays in the app, it has to become volleyball-specific: occlusion video of setters and attackers, and a quiet-eye serve routine done with a real ball. Generic dot tapping cannot produce honest skill claims. Because no study has tested a pre-match app package for flow or match performance, Flow of Ace could also generate its own evidence by logging pre-match state, post-match flow and serve percentage. That would make it one of the few products in this space able to back its claims with data.
